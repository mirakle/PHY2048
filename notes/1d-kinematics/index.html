Motion along a line

One-dimensional kinematics: how to describe where something is, how fast it moves, and how its motion changes. Read a short chunk, try the simulation, then test yourself.

Before you start: how to use these notes

Each part is broken into short chunks. After each chunk, pause and ask yourself: “Could I explain this to a classmate?” If not, reread it before moving on.

When you see a Predict box, write down your prediction before you run the simulation. Being surprised by a wrong prediction is one of the fastest ways to learn. Being right without committing first teaches much less.

The notes are split into pages. This page sets the ground rules; then each part has its own page:

  • Part 1: Describing motion and constant velocity
  • Part 2: Constant acceleration
  • Part 3: Variable acceleration (ends with a summary of all three parts)

Each part ends with six practice questions: three about ideas and three with calculations. Try each one honestly first. Hints come one at a time, and the full explanation opens after your first attempt.

Ground rules

  • Horizontal motion: the positive direction is to the right. We call the position \(x\).
  • Vertical motion: the positive direction is up. We call the position \(y\).
  • Velocity is \(v\) and acceleration is \(a\). In one dimension, the sign (+ or −) tells the direction, so we do not need arrows over the symbols.
  • Near Earth’s surface, the size of the free-fall acceleration is \(g = 9.80\ \text{m/s}^2\).
  • Time \(t\) starts at 0 when we start watching, unless a problem says otherwise.
Colour and line code used everywhere

Position is blue with a solid line, velocity is orange with a dashed line, and acceleration is green with a dash-dot line. Every graph also labels its axis in words, so you never need colour to tell them apart.

Part 1

Describing motion and constant velocity

By the end of Part 1 you should be able to

  • tell position, displacement, and distance apart;
  • draw and read a motion diagram;
  • find velocity as the slope of an \(x\)–\(t\) graph, and displacement as the area under a \(v\)–\(t\) graph;
  • use \(x = x_0 + v t\) to solve problems.

1.1 Position: where is it?

To say where something is, you need three choices: an origin (the zero), a positive direction, and a unit (we use metres). These choices make a coordinate axis, like a number line.

The position \(x\) is the reading on that number line where the object is.

Watch out

A negative position only means “on the negative side of the origin”. It tells you nothing about which way the object is moving, or how fast.

1.2 Displacement and distance

Displacement is the change in position: where you ended minus where you started.

\[ \Delta x = x_\text{f} - x_\text{i} \]

Displacement has a sign. A positive \(\Delta x\) means the object ended up to the right of where it started. A negative \(\Delta x\) means it ended up to the left.

Distance travelled is the total length of the path. It counts every metre you walked, in either direction, so it is never negative.

Key idea

Displacement only cares about the start and the end. Distance cares about the whole trip. They are equal only when the object never turns around.

1.3 Motion diagrams

Imagine taking a photo of a moving object at equal time steps (say every 1 s) and stacking all the photos on top of each other. Now replace the object with a dot. That is a motion diagram.

An arrow from one dot to the next shows the velocity during that time step. A longer arrow means the object covered more ground in the same time, so it was moving faster.

Stop and think

In diagram B, the dots are evenly spaced and the arrows point left. Is the velocity changing? (No: same size and same direction every second, so velocity is constant. “Negative” is not the same as “changing”.)

1.4 Velocity and speed

Average velocity is displacement divided by the time it took:

\[ v_\text{avg} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{x_\text{f} - x_\text{i}}{t_\text{f} - t_\text{i}} \]

Its unit is m/s. Its sign tells you the direction of the overall motion: positive means net motion to the right, negative means net motion to the left.

Average speed uses distance instead of displacement, so it is never negative:

\[ \text{average speed} = \frac{\text{distance travelled}}{\Delta t} \]

For the walk in Figure 1.2, if it took 6.0 s: average velocity is \(+3\ \text{m} / 6.0\ \text{s} = +0.50\ \text{m/s}\), but average speed is \(9\ \text{m} / 6.0\ \text{s} = 1.5\ \text{m/s}\). Same trip, different questions.

The instantaneous velocity is the velocity at one moment, which is what a speedometer (plus a direction) would show. When velocity is constant, the instantaneous and average velocities are the same number. Part 2 shows how to find instantaneous velocity when they differ.

1.5 Constant velocity: graphs and equation

If the velocity never changes, the object covers equal displacements in equal times. That gives two simple graphs.

Position vs. time is a straight line

The slope of an \(x\)–\(t\) graph is the velocity: rise (\(\Delta x\)) over run (\(\Delta t\)). A steeper line means a faster object. A line sloping down means a negative velocity.

Velocity vs. time is a flat line

The area between a \(v\)–\(t\) graph and the time axis is the displacement. For a flat line, that area is a rectangle: height \(v\) times width \(t\). Area below the axis counts as negative.

The equation

Rearranging \(v = \Delta x / \Delta t\) with a start time of zero gives the position at any time:

\[ x(t) = x_0 + v\,t \qquad (\text{constant } v) \]

Here \(x_0\) is the position at \(t = 0\). This is the equation of the straight line in Figure 1.4: intercept \(x_0\), slope \(v\).

Two classic traps with graphs

A graph is not a picture of the path. An \(x\)–\(t\) line that goes “uphill” does not mean the object is moving up a hill. It means \(x\) is increasing: the object moves right.

Height is not slope. On an \(x\)–\(t\) graph, the height of the line is where the object is. The steepness is how fast it moves. Two objects at the same position at the same instant (where their lines cross) can have very different velocities.

Part 2

Constant acceleration

By the end of Part 2 you should be able to

  • find instantaneous velocity as the slope of a tangent line;
  • decide the sign of acceleration from a motion diagram, and tell whether an object speeds up or slows down;
  • move between \(x\)–\(t\), \(v\)–\(t\) and \(a\)–\(t\) graphs using slopes and areas;
  • choose and use the right constant-acceleration equation, including for free fall.

2.1 When velocity changes: instantaneous velocity

When an object speeds up, its \(x\)–\(t\) graph curves. A curve has no single slope, so which “slope” is the velocity?

Pick a moment \(t_1\). Draw a straight line (a secant) from the graph at \(t_1\) to the graph a little later, at \(t_1 + \Delta t\). Its slope is the average velocity over that interval. Now shrink \(\Delta t\). The secant swings around and settles onto the tangent line, the line that just touches the curve at \(t_1\).

\[ v = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{dx}{dt} \]

The instantaneous velocity is the slope of the tangent line: the derivative of position with respect to time.

Explore: from average to instantaneous velocity

The cart’s position is \(x(t) = (1.00\ \text{m/s}^2)\,t^2\). Shrink \(\Delta t\) and watch the secant slope approach the tangent slope.

2.2 Acceleration

Acceleration tells you how quickly the velocity changes. It is not about how fast the object moves.

\[ a_\text{avg} = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} \qquad\qquad a = \frac{dv}{dt} \]

The unit is (m/s)/s, written m/s². An acceleration of \(+2\ \text{m/s}^2\) means the velocity goes up by 2 m/s every second.

Finding acceleration on a motion diagram

Take two velocity arrows in a row. Ask: “What arrow would I add to the first one to get the second?” That change, \(\Delta v\), points the same way as the acceleration.

Key idea: speeding up or slowing down?

Compare the signs of \(v\) and \(a\). Same sign: speeding up. Opposite signs: slowing down. A negative acceleration does not automatically mean “slowing down”. Diagram C has negative acceleration and is speeding up.

Watch out

Zero velocity does not mean zero acceleration. A ball thrown straight up has \(v = 0\) for an instant at the top, but its velocity is still changing (from up to down), so \(a = -9.80\ \text{m/s}^2\) right there.

2.3 Graphs of constant acceleration

If \(a\) is constant, the velocity changes by the same amount every second, so the \(v\)–\(t\) graph is a straight line with slope \(a\). The \(x\)–\(t\) graph is then a parabola: its slope (the velocity) changes steadily.

Key idea: slopes go down, areas go up

Slope of \(x\)–\(t\) = \(v\). Slope of \(v\)–\(t\) = \(a\). Going the other way: area under \(a\)–\(t\) = change in velocity \(\Delta v\). Area under \(v\)–\(t\) = displacement \(\Delta x\).

Check it on Figure 2.2. From 0 to 3 s, the area under the \(v\)–\(t\) line is a triangle: \(\tfrac12 (3\ \text{s})(6\ \text{m/s}) = 9\ \text{m}\). That matches the top of the parabola, \(x = 9\ \text{m}\). From 3 to 6 s, the triangle is below the axis (−9 m), so the ball is back at \(x = 0\) at 6 s.

2.4 The four constant-acceleration equations

Start from the \(v\)–\(t\) graph, a straight line from \(v_0\) at \(t = 0\) to \(v\) at time \(t\).

Slope. The slope is \(a\), so \(v - v_0 = a t\):

\[ v = v_0 + a t \tag{1} \]

Area. The area under the line is a rectangle (\(v_0 t\)) plus a triangle (\(\tfrac12 \cdot t \cdot a t\)):

\[ x = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 a t^2 \tag{2} \]

No time. Solve (1) for \(t\) and put it into (2):

\[ v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\,(x - x_0) \tag{3} \]

Average velocity. For a straight-line \(v\)–\(t\) graph, the average velocity is halfway between start and end:

\[ x - x_0 = \tfrac12 (v_0 + v)\,t \tag{4} \]
Pick the equation that leaves out the quantity you neither know nor want
EquationLeaves out
(1) \(v = v_0 + a t\)\(x - x_0\)
(2) \(x = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 a t^2\)\(v\)
(3) \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)\)\(t\)
(4) \(x - x_0 = \tfrac12 (v_0 + v)\,t\)\(a\)
Use only when \(a\) is constant

All four equations come from a straight \(v\)–\(t\) line. If the acceleration changes during the motion, they give wrong answers. Part 3 shows what to do instead.

2.5 Free fall

When air resistance is small, every object near Earth’s surface falls with the same constant acceleration, whatever its mass. With up as positive:

\[ a = -g = -9.80\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

This is true on the way up, at the top, and on the way down. “Free fall” means “only gravity acts”, so a ball thrown upward is in free fall as soon as it leaves your hand.

Two useful results follow from the equations with \(a = -g\), for a ball launched upward at speed \(v_0\):

  • Time to reach the top (set \(v = 0\) in equation 1): \(t_\text{top} = v_0 / g\).
  • Maximum rise (set \(v = 0\) in equation 3): \(h = v_0^2 / (2g)\).

If it lands at the launch height, the trip down takes the same time as the trip up, and it arrives with the same speed it left with, now pointing down.

2.6 A problem-solving routine

Experts rarely start by hunting for an equation. They first build a picture of the motion. Try these steps, in order:

  1. Sketch the situation. Mark the origin and the positive direction.
  2. Draw a motion diagram. Decide the signs of \(v\) and \(a\) from it.
  3. List knowns and unknowns in a table, with signs: \(x_0, x, v_0, v, a, t\).
  4. Choose an equation that leaves out the quantity you don’t know and don’t need.
  5. Solve with symbols first, then put in numbers with units.
  6. Check: right units? Sensible sign? Reasonable size?
Worked example

A car moving at 15 m/s sees a red light 30 m ahead and brakes steadily, stopping right at the line. Find its acceleration and the braking time.

Picture: origin where braking starts, right is positive. A motion diagram (like the one on the opening page) shows dots getting closer, moving right: \(v > 0\) and slowing, so \(a < 0\).

Knowns: \(x_0 = 0\), \(x = 30\ \text{m}\), \(v_0 = +15\ \text{m/s}\), \(v = 0\). Unknowns: \(a\), \(t\).

Acceleration: no \(t\) given, so use (3): \(a = \dfrac{v^2 - v_0^2}{2(x - x_0)} = \dfrac{0 - 225}{60}\ \text{m/s}^2 = -3.75\ \text{m/s}^2\).

Time: use (4), which avoids the \(a\) we just calculated, so a slip there cannot spread: \(t = \dfrac{2(x - x_0)}{v_0 + v} = \dfrac{60}{15}\ \text{s} = 4.00\ \text{s}\).

Check: \(a\) is negative, as the motion diagram said. Units work. Average speed 7.5 m/s for 4 s gives 30 m. ✓

Part 3

Variable acceleration

By the end of Part 3 you should be able to

  • explain why the constant-acceleration equations fail when \(a\) changes;
  • find \(v(t)\) and \(a(t)\) from \(x(t)\) by taking derivatives;
  • find \(v(t)\) and \(x(t)\) from \(a(t)\) by integrating, using initial conditions;
  • read the same relationships from graphs as slopes, curvature, and areas.

3.1 When the constant-acceleration equations fail

Real accelerations change all the time: you ease off the gas pedal, a rocket burns its fuel, a spring stretches, air drag grows with speed.

Here is a test. An e-bike starts from rest. Its acceleration fades steadily as the motor eases off:

\[ a(t) = 2.0\ \text{m/s}^2 - (0.50\ \text{m/s}^3)\,t \qquad (0 \le t \le 4.0\ \text{s}) \]

What is its velocity at \(t = 4.0\ \text{s}\)? Two tempting shortcuts:

  • Use the starting acceleration in \(v = v_0 + a t\): \(v = 0 + (2.0)(4.0) = 8.0\ \text{m/s}\). Wrong: the acceleration does not stay at 2.0 m/s².
  • Use the final acceleration: \(a(4.0) = 0\), so \(v = 0\). Wrong: the bike clearly sped up.

The correct answer is 4.0 m/s. Section 3.3 shows how to get it. The lesson for now: with changing acceleration, you must account for the acceleration at every instant, not plug one value into a formula built for a constant.

3.2 Going down: derivatives

The definitions from Part 2 work for any motion, constant or not:

\[ v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} \qquad\qquad a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} \]

If you know the position as a function of time, differentiate once for velocity and twice for acceleration.

Example

A particle moves with \(x(t) = (1.0)\,t^3 - (6.0)\,t^2 + (9.0)\,t\), with \(x\) in metres and \(t\) in seconds. Then

\[ v(t) = 3.0\,t^2 - 12\,t + 9.0 = 3.0\,(t - 1)(t - 3) \qquad a(t) = 6.0\,t - 12 \]
  • The particle stops momentarily (\(v = 0\)) at \(t = 1.0\ \text{s}\) and \(t = 3.0\ \text{s}\). These are the peak and valley of the \(x\)–\(t\) graph.
  • The acceleration is zero at \(t = 2.0\ \text{s}\). There the \(x\)–\(t\) graph switches from bending down to bending up, and the velocity is at its most negative.
Reading curvature

The way an \(x\)–\(t\) graph bends tells you the sign of \(a\). Bending upward (like a cup that holds water): \(a > 0\). Bending downward (like an upside-down cup): \(a < 0\). A straight piece: \(a = 0\).

3.3 Going up: integrals

Often we know the acceleration (from forces, or an accelerometer) and want the velocity and position. Run the derivatives backwards: integrate. The area under \(a\)–\(t\) from 0 to \(t\) is the change in velocity, and the area under \(v\)–\(t\) is the change in position:

\[ v(t) = v_0 + \int_0^t a(t')\,dt' \qquad\qquad x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t v(t')\,dt' \]

The starting values \(v_0\) and \(x_0\) are the initial conditions. Integrating only tells you how much something changed, so you always need to know where you started.

Worked example: the e-bike, done right

\(a(t) = 2.0 - 0.50\,t\), starting from rest at \(x_0 = 0\).

Velocity: \(v(t) = 0 + \int_0^t (2.0 - 0.50\,t')\,dt' = 2.0\,t - 0.25\,t^2\). At \(t = 4.0\ \text{s}\): \(v = 8.0 - 4.0 = 4.0\ \text{m/s}\).

Position: \(x(t) = 0 + \int_0^t (2.0\,t' - 0.25\,t'^2)\,dt' = 1.0\,t^2 - 0.0833\,t^3\). At \(t = 4.0\ \text{s}\): \(x = 16 - 5.3 = 10.7\ \text{m}\).

Check with a graph: \(a\)–\(t\) is a straight line from 2.0 down to 0 over 4.0 s. The area under it is a triangle, \(\tfrac12 (4.0)(2.0) = 4.0\ \text{m/s}\). ✓

Where the constant-acceleration equations come from

Put \(a(t) = a\) (a constant) into the integrals:

\[ v(t) = v_0 + \int_0^t a\,dt' = v_0 + a t \] \[ x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t (v_0 + a t')\,dt' = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 a t^2 \]

These are equations (1) and (2) from Part 2. The constant-acceleration equations are just one special case of the general integrals.

3.4 Acceleration that depends on position or velocity

Sometimes the acceleration is given in terms of \(x\) or \(v\), not \(t\):

  • A glider on a spring: \(a = -(k/m)\,x\). The farther it is stretched, the harder it is pulled back.
  • A ball with air drag: the drag grows with speed, for example \(a = -c\,v\,|v|\) for a coasting object.

You cannot integrate “\(a\,dt\)” directly, because you don’t yet know \(x(t)\) or \(v(t)\). A computer gets around this by taking tiny time steps \(\Delta t\):

\[ v_\text{new} = v + a\,\Delta t \qquad\qquad x_\text{new} = x + v\,\Delta t \]

Recompute \(a\) from the new \(x\) and \(v\), and repeat. Each step is a small piece of the area under a graph. Simulation 3 uses a more careful version of this idea (called fourth-order Runge–Kutta), the same method behind the course’s drop tower simulator with air drag.

Big ideas to carry forward

  • Position, velocity, and acceleration are different quantities. Knowing one does not tell you the others at the same instant.
  • Signs carry direction. Speeding up means \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign.
  • Slopes take you down the ladder (\(x \to v \to a\)); areas take you up (\(a \to v \to x\)).
  • The four constant-acceleration equations are a special case. Check that \(a\) is constant before you use them.

For instructors: how physics education research shaped these notes
  • Targeted difficulties. Many questions probe confusions documented by Trowbridge and McDermott: mixing up position and velocity (“same position means same speed”) and velocity and acceleration (“zero velocity means zero acceleration”).
  • Graph interpretation. The slope-versus-height and graph-as-picture traps come from McDermott, Rosenquist and van Zee, and from Beichner’s Test of Understanding Graphs in Kinematics.
  • Predict, observe, explain. Prediction prompts before each simulation follow the Interactive Lecture Demonstration approach of Sokoloff and Thornton, and the elicit–confront–resolve cycle.
  • Multiple representations. Every idea appears in words, motion diagrams, graphs and equations, following Van Heuvelen. The problem routine asks for a qualitative picture before any equation.
  • Feedback on reasoning. Each wrong answer gets feedback aimed at the specific idea behind it, and conceptual questions include a space to write reasoning, which suits peer discussion (Mazur’s Peer Instruction). Hake’s survey showed large gains from this kind of interactive engagement.
  • Accessibility. Colour is always paired with line style, arrowhead shape or a text label; every graphic has a text description; simulations have keyboard controls, text readouts and data tables.

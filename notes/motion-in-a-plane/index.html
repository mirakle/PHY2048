Motion in a plane

Vectors, projectiles, circular motion and relative motion. Each idea gets a picture first, then the vectors and trigonometry.

Before you start: how to use these notes

These notes continue the notes on motion along a line. You should be comfortable with position, velocity and acceleration in one dimension, and with the constant-acceleration equations.

Each part is split into short chunks. Read one, then ask yourself: “Could I explain this to a classmate?” When you see a Predict box, commit to a prediction before you touch the simulation.

The notes are split into pages:

  • Part 1: Vectors, in one and two dimensions, with trigonometry and unit vectors
  • Part 2: Projectile motion, with optional challenge questions
  • Part 3: Circular motion, uniform and non-uniform
  • Part 4: Relative motion, with an optional look at what happens near the speed of light

Each part ends with six practice questions: three about ideas and three with calculations. Hints come one at a time, and the worked explanation opens after your first attempt. Longer derivations sit behind “Show the derivation” links so they don’t interrupt the main story.

Ground rules

  • The +x axis points to the right (or east) and the +y axis points up (or north, on a map).
  • Unless a problem says otherwise, an angle \(\theta\) is measured counterclockwise from the +x axis. A problem may also say “30° above the horizontal” or “20° east of north”; always sketch it.
  • A vector is written with an arrow, \(\vec A\). Its magnitude (length) is \(|\vec A|\) or just \(A\). Its components are \(A_x\) and \(A_y\).
  • The unit vectors \(\hat\imath\) and \(\hat\jmath\) have length 1 and point along +x and +y. So \(\vec A = A_x\,\hat\imath + A_y\,\hat\jmath\).
  • Position is \(\vec r = x\,\hat\imath + y\,\hat\jmath\), velocity is \(\vec v\), acceleration is \(\vec a\).
  • Near Earth’s surface, \(g = 9.80\ \text{m/s}^2\) and the free-fall acceleration is \(\vec a = -g\,\hat\jmath\).
Colour and line code

Position is blue, velocity is orange with a filled arrowhead, and acceleration is green with an open arrowhead. For general vectors in Part 1: A is plum and solid, B is teal and dashed, and the resultant R is black and thick. Every arrow is also labelled, so you never need colour to tell them apart.

Part 1

Vectors

By the end of Part 1 you should be able to

  • tell scalars from vectors, and treat displacement, velocity and acceleration as vectors;
  • add and subtract vectors by drawing them tail to tip;
  • find components with sine and cosine, and get back the magnitude and direction;
  • write vectors with \(\hat\imath\) and \(\hat\jmath\), and add them by components.

1.1 Scalars and vectors

Some quantities are fully described by a number and a unit: a time of 3.0 s, a mass of 2.0 kg, a distance of 40 m. These are scalars.

Others also need a direction. “Walk 40 m” is not enough to find the café; “walk 40 m north” is. Quantities with a size and a direction are vectors.

Scalar and vector partners
Scalar (size only)Vector (size and direction)
distance travelleddisplacement \(\Delta\vec r\)
speedvelocity \(\vec v\)
(no common scalar partner)acceleration \(\vec a\)
time, mass, temperature(none)

1.2 Vectors in one dimension

Along a line there are only two directions, so a sign does the job of the direction: + means toward +x, − means toward −x. You already used one-dimensional vectors in the earlier notes: \(\Delta x\), \(v\) and \(a\) all carried signs.

Draw each vector as an arrow. To add two of them, place the tail of the second at the tip of the first. The sum (the resultant) goes from the first tail to the last tip.

1.3 Vectors in two dimensions

On a plane, a sign is no longer enough: a vector can point in any direction. We describe it either by its magnitude and direction (“5 m at 37°”), or by its components (“4 m along x and 3 m along y”). Section 1.6 shows how to switch between the two.

Three rules about arrows

  • Moving an arrow does not change the vector, as long as you keep its length and direction. That is what lets us slide arrows tail to tip.
  • The negative \(-\vec A\) has the same length as \(\vec A\) and points the opposite way.
  • Multiplying by a number stretches the arrow: \(2\vec A\) is twice as long, same direction. A negative number also flips it. This is how a velocity becomes a displacement: \(\Delta\vec r = \vec v\,\Delta t\).

1.4 Adding vectors graphically

Tail to tip

Same rule as in one dimension: slide \(\vec B\) so its tail sits on the tip of \(\vec A\). The resultant \(\vec R = \vec A + \vec B\) runs from the tail of \(\vec A\) to the tip of \(\vec B\).

The parallelogram picture

Draw both vectors from the same point and complete the parallelogram. The diagonal is \(\vec A + \vec B\). It is the same arrow as before, and it shows that order does not matter: \(\vec A + \vec B = \vec B + \vec A\).

Watch out

Lengths do not simply add. In Figure 1.3, \(|\vec A| \approx 4.1\) and \(|\vec B| \approx 3.2\), but \(|\vec R| \approx 6.4\), not 7.3. Lengths add only when the vectors point the same way.

Subtracting

Subtracting means adding the opposite: \(\vec A - \vec B = \vec A + (-\vec B)\). Or, with the tails together, \(\vec A - \vec B\) runs from the tip of B to the tip of A: it is the vector you add to \(\vec B\) to get \(\vec A\).

Vector workspace: add and subtract by dragging

Predict. Keep \(\vec A\) fixed. How must you point \(\vec B\) to make \(|\vec A + \vec B|\) as large as possible? As small as possible? Try it, then switch the view to Components and watch \(R_x\) and \(R_y\).

1.5 The trigonometry you need

Every 2-D vector, with its components, forms a right triangle. Three ratios link the angle \(\theta\) to the sides.

\[ \sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} \]

And Pythagoras: \(\text{hypotenuse}^2 = \text{adjacent}^2 + \text{opposite}^2\).

To go backwards from sides to angle, use the inverse functions, for example \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(\text{opposite}/\text{adjacent})\).

The calculator trap

A calculator’s \(\tan^{-1}\) only returns angles between −90° and +90°. For a vector pointing left (negative x component), it gives an angle in the wrong half of the plane. Always sketch the vector and decide its quadrant. If \(A_x < 0\), add 180° to the calculator’s answer.

Show a table of useful values
Sine and cosine of common angles

Notice that \(\sin\theta = \cos(90° - \theta)\). That is why the sine of an angle is the cosine of the other acute angle in the triangle.

1.6 Components and unit vectors

From magnitude and direction to components

With \(\theta\) measured counterclockwise from +x, the formulas work in every quadrant (the signs come out automatically):

\[ A_x = A\cos\theta \qquad\qquad A_y = A\sin\theta \]

From components back to magnitude and direction

\[ A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2} \qquad\qquad \tan\theta = \frac{A_y}{A_x}\ \ (\text{then check the quadrant}) \]

Unit-vector notation

The unit vectors \(\hat\imath\) and \(\hat\jmath\) are arrows of length 1 along +x and +y. Any vector is a sum of a stretched \(\hat\imath\) and a stretched \(\hat\jmath\):

\[ \vec A = A_x\,\hat\imath + A_y\,\hat\jmath \]

For example, \(\vec A = 4\,\hat\imath + 3\,\hat\jmath\) means “4 along x, then 3 along y”: the vector in Figure 1.2.

Adding by components

To add vectors, add the \(\hat\imath\) parts and the \(\hat\jmath\) parts separately:

\[ \vec A + \vec B = (A_x + B_x)\,\hat\imath + (A_y + B_y)\,\hat\jmath \]
Worked example: a drone’s trip

A drone flies 120 m at 30.0°, then 80.0 m at 135° (angles from +x). Find its total displacement.

Components. \(\vec d_1 = 120\cos 30.0°\,\hat\imath + 120\sin 30.0°\,\hat\jmath = (103.9\,\hat\imath + 60.0\,\hat\jmath)\ \text{m}\). \(\vec d_2 = 80.0\cos 135°\,\hat\imath + 80.0\sin 135°\,\hat\jmath = (-56.6\,\hat\imath + 56.6\,\hat\jmath)\ \text{m}\).

Add. \(\vec R = (47.3\,\hat\imath + 116.6\,\hat\jmath)\ \text{m}\).

Magnitude and direction. \(R = \sqrt{47.3^2 + 116.6^2} = 126\ \text{m}\). Both components are positive (first quadrant), so \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(116.6/47.3) = 67.9°\).

Check with a sketch: the second leg points up and to the left, so the result should lean left of the first leg but still point into the first quadrant. ✓

1.7 Displacement, velocity and acceleration as vectors

Everything from the 1-D notes carries over, one component at a time.

  • Position \(\vec r = x\,\hat\imath + y\,\hat\jmath\). Displacement \(\Delta\vec r = \vec r_2 - \vec r_1\).
  • Velocity \(\vec v = \dfrac{d\vec r}{dt} = v_x\,\hat\imath + v_y\,\hat\jmath\), with \(v_x = dx/dt\) and \(v_y = dy/dt\). It is always tangent to the path.
  • Acceleration \(\vec a = \dfrac{d\vec v}{dt}\). On average, \(\vec a_\text{avg} = \Delta\vec v/\Delta t\).
Key idea

The acceleration points along the change in velocity, not along the velocity. An object can accelerate without changing speed, just by turning. This idea drives Parts 2 and 3.

Part 2

Projectile motion

By the end of Part 2 you should be able to

  • explain why horizontal and vertical motion can be treated separately;
  • describe the velocity and acceleration at any point of the flight, including the top;
  • split an initial velocity into components and use the constant-acceleration equations for each;
  • find the time of flight, maximum height and range, and know when the level-ground formulas apply.

2.1 The big picture

A projectile is anything moving under gravity alone once it is launched: a thrown ball, a jumping cat, water from a hose. We ignore air resistance throughout.

Predict. From the same height, at the same instant, one ball is dropped and another is thrown sideways. Which hits the floor first? Decide before you look at Figure 2.1.

Figure 2.1 shows the three ideas that explain all of projectile motion.

Three key ideas

1. The two directions are independent. Sideways motion does not change how fast something falls, and falling does not change how fast it moves sideways.

2. Horizontally: constant velocity. Nothing pushes sideways, so \(v_x\) never changes. Equal sideways steps in equal times.

3. Vertically: free fall. Exactly the 1-D free fall you already know, with \(a_y = -g\). It speeds up going down and slows down going up.

Put a steady sideways motion together with a free fall and you get a curved path: a parabola.

Two common ideas that are wrong

“At the top, the velocity is zero.” Only the vertical part is zero. The ball is still moving sideways at \(v_x\), so the velocity at the top is horizontal.

“The throw keeps pushing the ball for a while.” Once the ball leaves your hand, only gravity acts. The acceleration is \(9.80\ \text{m/s}^2\) straight down at every point, from launch to landing, including the top.

2.2 Setting up with vectors

Put the origin at the launch point (or on the ground below it), +x along the horizontal motion and +y up. Then

\[ \vec a = 0\,\hat\imath - g\,\hat\jmath \]

Split the launch velocity (speed \(v_0\), angle \(\theta\) above the horizontal) into components, as in Part 1:

\[ v_{0x} = v_0\cos\theta \qquad\qquad v_{0y} = v_0\sin\theta \]

Now write the 1-D equations twice, once per axis.

The projectile equations
Horizontal (\(a_x = 0\))Vertical (\(a_y = -g\))
\(v_x = v_{0x}\)\(v_y = v_{0y} - g t\)
\(x = x_0 + v_{0x}\,t\)\(y = y_0 + v_{0y}\,t - \tfrac12 g t^2\)
\(v_y^2 = v_{0y}^2 - 2g\,(y - y_0)\)

In vector form, the same information reads \(\vec r(t) = \vec r_0 + \vec v_0\,t + \tfrac12 \vec a\,t^2\) and \(\vec v(t) = \vec v_0 + \vec a\,t\). The time \(t\) is the only quantity shared by both columns. It is usually the link that connects the two directions in a problem.

At any instant the velocity is \(\vec v = v_x\,\hat\imath + v_y\,\hat\jmath\), with speed \(\sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}\) and direction \(\tan^{-1}(v_y/v_x)\) relative to the horizontal.

2.3 Results for level ground

When the projectile lands at the same height it was launched from, three results are worth knowing:

\[ t_\text{top} = \frac{v_0\sin\theta}{g} \qquad H = \frac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{2g} \qquad R = \frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g} \]

\(t_\text{top}\) is the time to reach the top, \(H\) the maximum height, and \(R\) the range (horizontal distance at landing). The total flight time is \(2t_\text{top}\).

Show the derivations

Time to the top. At the top \(v_y = 0\): \(0 = v_0\sin\theta - g\,t_\text{top}\), so \(t_\text{top} = v_0\sin\theta / g\).

Maximum height. Use \(v_y^2 = v_{0y}^2 - 2g\,\Delta y\) with \(v_y = 0\): \(H = \dfrac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}\).

Range. Landing at the launch height: \(0 = v_{0y}T - \tfrac12 g T^2\), so \(T = 2v_0\sin\theta/g = 2t_\text{top}\). Then \(R = v_{0x}T = \dfrac{2v_0^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{g} = \dfrac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}\), using \(2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sin 2\theta\).

Best angle. \(\sin 2\theta\) is largest (equal to 1) when \(2\theta = 90°\), so \(\theta = 45°\) gives the longest range. Because \(\sin(180° - 2\theta) = \sin 2\theta\), the angles \(\theta\) and \(90° - \theta\) give the same range.

Use with care

These three formulas only work when the landing height equals the launch height (and without air resistance). For a cliff, a balcony or a hoop, go back to the equations in Section 2.2.

2.4 Worked examples

Example A: launched horizontally

A tennis ball is served horizontally from a height of 2.40 m at 30.0 m/s. Where does it land, and with what velocity?

Vertical (sets the time). \(v_{0y} = 0\), \(\Delta y = -2.40\ \text{m}\): \(-2.40 = -\tfrac12 (9.80)\,t^2 \Rightarrow t = 0.700\ \text{s}\).

Horizontal. \(x = (30.0)(0.700) = 21.0\ \text{m}\).

Velocity at landing. \(v_x = 30.0\ \text{m/s}\), \(v_y = -(9.80)(0.700) = -6.86\ \text{m/s}\). So \(\vec v = (30.0\,\hat\imath - 6.86\,\hat\jmath)\ \text{m/s}\): speed 30.8 m/s, at \(\tan^{-1}(6.86/30.0) = 12.9°\) below the horizontal.

Example B: launched at an angle

A firework shell leaves the ground at 35.0 m/s, 70.0° above the horizontal. When and where does it reach its highest point?

Components. \(v_{0x} = 35.0\cos 70.0° = 12.0\ \text{m/s}\), \(v_{0y} = 35.0\sin 70.0° = 32.9\ \text{m/s}\).

Time to the top (\(v_y = 0\)): \(t = 32.9/9.80 = 3.36\ \text{s}\).

Height: \(H = \dfrac{(32.9)^2}{2(9.80)} = 55.2\ \text{m}\). Horizontal position: \(x = (12.0)(3.36) = 40.2\ \text{m}\).

Check: at the top, the velocity is \(12.0\,\hat\imath\ \text{m/s}\): horizontal, not zero. ✓

2.5 A routine for projectile problems

  1. Sketch the path. Choose the origin and mark +x and +y.
  2. Split the launch velocity into \(v_{0x}\) and \(v_{0y}\), with signs.
  3. Make two lists of knowns, one for x (where \(a_x = 0\)) and one for y (where \(a_y = -g\)).
  4. Find the time from whichever direction has enough information. Usually that is y.
  5. Use that time in the other direction.
  6. Check: signs, units, and whether the size makes sense.

Simulation: projectile launcher

Set the launch speed, angle and height. Grey dots mark the ball’s position at equal time steps. The four graphs show each direction separately: a straight line for \(x\), a parabola for \(y\), a constant \(v_x\) and a steadily falling \(v_y\).

Predict. Set the angle to 0° and the height to 20 m, and turn on “Drop a second ball”. Will the two balls land together? Then try 30° and 60° with zero height: which lands farther? Which stays up longer?

Part 3

Circular motion

By the end of Part 3 you should be able to

  • explain why an object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating, and which way;
  • use \(v = r\omega = 2\pi r/T\) and \(a_c = v^2/r = \omega^2 r\);
  • describe circular motion as two perpendicular oscillations, \(x = r\cos\omega t\) and \(y = r\sin\omega t\);
  • combine tangential and centripetal acceleration when the speed changes.

3.1 The big picture

A car rounding a curve, a satellite in orbit, a point on a spinning wheel: each moves along a circle. Start with the simplest case, uniform circular motion, where the speed stays constant.

Predict. A car drives around a roundabout at a steady 30 km/h. Is it accelerating? If yes, in which direction?

Recall from Part 1: acceleration measures how fast the velocity vector changes. Here the speed stays the same, but the direction of the velocity changes all the time. So the car is accelerating.

Key ideas

Velocity is always tangent to the circle.

Acceleration always points to the centre. It is called centripetal (“centre-seeking”) acceleration. It changes the direction of the velocity, not its size.

Faster or tighter turns need more acceleration. The size is \(a_c = v^2/r\) (Section 3.3).

Watch out

Nothing pulls an object “outward”. If whatever keeps it on the circle (a string, friction, gravity) disappears, the object does not fly outward along the radius. It continues in a straight line along the tangent, with the velocity it had at that moment.

3.2 Describing circular motion

On a circle of radius \(r\), one number pins down where the object is: the angle \(\theta\) from the +x axis. We measure it in radians, so that the distance along the arc is simply

\[ s = r\,\theta \qquad (\theta \text{ in radians; one turn} = 2\pi\ \text{rad} = 360°) \]
  • Angular velocity \(\omega = d\theta/dt\), in rad/s.
  • Period \(T\): time for one full turn. Frequency \(f = 1/T\), in turns per second (Hz).
  • Speed \(v = ds/dt = r\,\omega\). Over one turn: \(v = 2\pi r/T\), and \(\omega = 2\pi/T = 2\pi f\).

3.3 Where centripetal acceleration comes from

Pick two moments a short time \(\Delta t\) apart. The velocity has the same length at both, but it has turned through the same angle \(\Delta\theta\) as the radius. Move the two velocity arrows tail to tail and draw \(\Delta\vec v\) from the first tip to the second.

Use the tool below to shrink \(\Delta\theta\). Watch two things:

  • \(\Delta\vec v\) (and so \(\vec a_\text{avg}\)) points closer and closer to straight at the centre;
  • \(|\Delta\vec v|/\Delta t\) settles on the value \(v^2/r\).

Explore: the change in velocity on a circle

Radius 2.0 m, speed 3.0 m/s, so \(v^2/r = 4.50\ \text{m/s}^2\). Left: the path. Right: the two velocities drawn from one point.

The result, for uniform circular motion:

\[ a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r \qquad \text{directed toward the centre} \]
Show the derivation (similar triangles)

The position vectors \(\vec r_1, \vec r_2\) and the velocity vectors \(\vec v_1, \vec v_2\) are each pairs of equal-length vectors separated by the same angle \(\Delta\theta\), because each velocity is perpendicular to its radius. So the triangle formed by \(\vec r_1, \vec r_2, \Delta\vec r\) and the triangle formed by \(\vec v_1, \vec v_2, \Delta\vec v\) are similar (both isosceles with the same apex angle):

\[ \frac{|\Delta\vec v|}{v} = \frac{|\Delta\vec r|}{r} \]

Divide by \(\Delta t\): \(\dfrac{|\Delta\vec v|}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{v}{r}\,\dfrac{|\Delta\vec r|}{\Delta t}\). As \(\Delta t \to 0\), \(|\Delta\vec r|/\Delta t \to v\) (the chord becomes the arc), so \(a = v \cdot v/r = v^2/r\).

Direction: \(\Delta\vec v\) is perpendicular to the chord between the two positions, and points inward. As \(\Delta t \to 0\), it points straight at the centre.

Exactly, for a finite angle: \(|\Delta\vec v| = 2v\sin(\Delta\theta/2)\) and \(\Delta t = r\Delta\theta/v\), so \(a_\text{avg} = \dfrac{v^2}{r}\cdot\dfrac{\sin(\Delta\theta/2)}{\Delta\theta/2}\). The last factor tends to 1 as \(\Delta\theta \to 0\). That is what the tool shows.

3.4 Two oscillations make a circle

Shine a light from above onto an object going around a circle, and watch its shadow on the floor. The shadow slides back and forth along a line: it oscillates. A light from the side gives a second shadow, oscillating up and down. Each shadow is one component of the position:

\[ \vec r(t) = r\cos(\omega t)\,\hat\imath + r\sin(\omega t)\,\hat\jmath \]

So uniform circular motion is two oscillations at right angles, with the same size and the same period, a quarter of a turn out of step (cosine and sine). The \(x\) shadow is fastest when it crosses the middle, and stops and turns around at the ends. The same is true of the \(y\) shadow.

Simulation: a circle and its two shadows

Predict. When the point is at the very top of the circle, what is the \(x\) shadow doing: moving fastest, or stopped? What about the \(y\) shadow?

Differentiate each component (the chain rule brings out a factor \(\omega\) each time):

\[ \vec v(t) = -r\omega\sin(\omega t)\,\hat\imath + r\omega\cos(\omega t)\,\hat\jmath \] \[ \vec a(t) = -r\omega^2\cos(\omega t)\,\hat\imath - r\omega^2\sin(\omega t)\,\hat\jmath = -\omega^2\,\vec r(t) \]
Key result

\(\vec a = -\omega^2\vec r\): the acceleration is always opposite to the position vector (toward the centre) and has size \(\omega^2 r = v^2/r\). The calculus gives the same answer as the geometry of Section 3.3.

Show the derivation and the checks

With \(x = r\cos\omega t\): \(v_x = dx/dt = -r\omega\sin\omega t\), and \(a_x = dv_x/dt = -r\omega^2\cos\omega t = -\omega^2 x\). Likewise \(y = r\sin\omega t\) gives \(v_y = r\omega\cos\omega t\) and \(a_y = -\omega^2 y\).

Speed: \(|\vec v| = r\omega\sqrt{\sin^2\omega t + \cos^2\omega t} = r\omega\). Constant, as it should be.

Velocity is tangent: \(\vec v \cdot \vec r = (-r\omega\sin\omega t)(r\cos\omega t) + (r\omega\cos\omega t)(r\sin\omega t) = 0\), so \(\vec v\) is perpendicular to the radius.

Acceleration: \(|\vec a| = \omega^2 r = (v/r)^2 r = v^2/r\), and \(\vec a = -\omega^2\vec r\) points from the object to the centre.

Looking ahead: each shadow obeys \(a_x = -\omega^2 x\). Later in the course, a mass on a spring obeys the same equation. That is why its motion is called simple harmonic motion: it is the shadow of a circle.

3.5 Non-uniform circular motion

Now let the speed change: a car speeding up out of a bend, or a Ferris wheel slowing to a stop. Split the acceleration into two perpendicular parts:

  • the tangential part \(a_t = \dfrac{dv}{dt}\), along the path. It changes the speed: forward when speeding up, backward when slowing down;
  • the centripetal part \(a_c = \dfrac{v^2}{r}\), toward the centre. It changes the direction, just as before.
\[ \vec a = \vec a_t + \vec a_c \qquad\qquad |\vec a| = \sqrt{a_t^2 + a_c^2} \]
Show the derivation

Let the angle \(\theta(t)\) be any function of time. Write \(\hat r = \cos\theta\,\hat\imath + \sin\theta\,\hat\jmath\) (outward unit vector) and \(\hat t = -\sin\theta\,\hat\imath + \cos\theta\,\hat\jmath\) (unit vector along the motion). Then \(d\hat r/dt = \omega\,\hat t\) and \(d\hat t/dt = -\omega\,\hat r\), with \(\omega = d\theta/dt\).

Position: \(\vec r = r\,\hat r\). Velocity: \(\vec v = r\omega\,\hat t = v\,\hat t\), tangent as always.

Acceleration (product rule): \(\vec a = \dfrac{dv}{dt}\,\hat t + v\,\dfrac{d\hat t}{dt} = \dfrac{dv}{dt}\,\hat t - v\omega\,\hat r = a_t\,\hat t - \dfrac{v^2}{r}\,\hat r\).

The first term is the tangential acceleration; the second is the centripetal acceleration, pointing toward the centre (\(-\hat r\)). If \(v\) is constant, the first term vanishes and we recover uniform circular motion.

Simulation: speeding up and slowing down on a circle

Predict. Start from rest with a positive \(a_t\). At the very start (\(v = 0\)), which way does the acceleration point? Later, as the car gets faster, does the total acceleration turn toward the centre or away from it?

3.6 Worked examples

Example A: Earth’s orbit

Earth orbits the Sun on a nearly circular path of radius \(1.50 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m}\), once every 365 days. Find its speed and centripetal acceleration.

\(T = 365 \times 86\,400\ \text{s} = 3.15 \times 10^7\ \text{s}\). Speed: \(v = 2\pi r/T = 2.99 \times 10^4\ \text{m/s}\) (about 30 km/s).

\(a_c = v^2/r = (2.99 \times 10^4)^2 / (1.50 \times 10^{11}) = 5.96 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m/s}^2\), toward the Sun. Huge speed, tiny acceleration, because the circle is so large.

Example B: a Ferris wheel slowing down

A rider on a Ferris wheel of radius 12.0 m moves at 3.00 m/s while the wheel slows at 0.500 m/s². Find the rider’s total acceleration.

\(a_c = v^2/r = 9.00/12.0 = 0.750\ \text{m/s}^2\) toward the centre. \(a_t = -0.500\ \text{m/s}^2\) (backward, opposite the velocity).

\(|\vec a| = \sqrt{0.750^2 + 0.500^2} = 0.901\ \text{m/s}^2\), tilted \(\tan^{-1}(0.500/0.750) = 33.7°\) from the inward radius, toward the back.

Part 4

Relative motion

By the end of Part 4 you should be able to

  • explain what a frame of reference is, and why different observers measure different velocities;
  • read and write the subscript notation \(\vec r_{O/2} = \vec r_{O/1} + \vec r_{1/2}\);
  • add relative velocities along a line and as vectors in a plane;
  • (optional) explain why this rule fails near the speed of light, and how relativity fixes it.

4.1 The big picture

You sit still in a moving train, reading. To you, the book is at rest. To someone on the platform, the book moves at 20 m/s. Who is right? Both. Position and velocity only have meaning relative to something.

A frame of reference is that “something”: an observer with an origin, a set of axes and a clock. The train is one frame; the platform is another. Each frame measures positions and velocities from its own origin.

Key ideas

Different frames can disagree about position, velocity and the shape of a path.

Frames moving at a constant velocity relative to each other agree about acceleration, and about the time between events (at everyday speeds).

No such frame is “the real one”. We pick whichever makes a problem easiest.

4.2 Relative position on a line

We need a notation that says what is measured and from where. We write

The subscript notation
xO/2position of the object O, measured in frame 2 (from the origin of frame 2)
xO/1position of the object O, measured in frame 1
x1/2position of frame 1 (its origin), measured in frame 2

Read the slash as “relative to”. Then, as Figure 4.1 shows, the positions simply add:

\[ x_{O/2} = x_{O/1} + x_{1/2} \]
How to check the subscripts

The labels chain together: \(O/\underline{1}\) then \(\underline{1}/2\) gives \(O/2\). The inner label (here 1) appears twice and “cancels”. If the inner labels don’t match, the equation is wrong.

Swapping the labels flips the sign: \(x_{2/1} = -x_{1/2}\). (If the train’s origin is 8 m ahead of the platform’s, the platform’s origin is 8 m behind the train’s.)

4.3 Relative velocity and acceleration

Differentiate the position equation with respect to time. Each term becomes a velocity, with the same subscripts:

\[ v_{O/2} = v_{O/1} + v_{1/2} \]

In words: velocity of O relative to frame 2 = velocity of O relative to frame 1 + velocity of frame 1 relative to frame 2. This is the Galilean rule for adding velocities.

Example: walking on a train

A train moves at \(v_{T/G} = +20.0\ \text{m/s}\) relative to the ground. A passenger walks toward the front at \(v_{P/T} = +1.5\ \text{m/s}\) relative to the train. Relative to the ground: \(v_{P/G} = v_{P/T} + v_{T/G} = +21.5\ \text{m/s}\).

Walking toward the back instead: \(v_{P/T} = -1.5\ \text{m/s}\), so \(v_{P/G} = +18.5\ \text{m/s}\). Still moving forward relative to the ground, just more slowly.

Differentiate once more: \(a_{O/2} = a_{O/1} + a_{1/2}\). If frame 1 moves at a constant velocity relative to frame 2, then \(a_{1/2} = 0\) and

\[ a_{O/2} = a_{O/1} \]

Both observers measure the same acceleration. That is why a ball tossed in a smoothly moving train falls with \(g\) for everyone. Frames like these are called inertial frames.

4.4 Relative motion in two dimensions

In a plane, the same rule holds with vectors. The subscripts chain in exactly the same way:

\[ \vec r_{O/2} = \vec r_{O/1} + \vec r_{1/2} \qquad \vec v_{O/2} = \vec v_{O/1} + \vec v_{1/2} \qquad \vec a_{O/2} = \vec a_{O/1}\ \ (\text{if } \vec v_{1/2} \text{ is constant}) \]

Velocities add the same way. The classic cases are a boat in a current and a plane in the wind. The trick is to label each velocity carefully:

  • a boat’s engine sets its velocity relative to the water, \(\vec v_{B/W}\);
  • the current is the water’s velocity relative to the ground, \(\vec v_{W/G}\);
  • what you see from the shore is \(\vec v_{B/G} = \vec v_{B/W} + \vec v_{W/G}\).
Worked example: flying due north in a crosswind

A plane has an airspeed of 200 km/h. A wind blows toward the east at 60.0 km/h. Which way must the pilot point the plane to travel due north, and what is its ground speed?

Set up. \(\vec v_{P/G} = \vec v_{P/A} + \vec v_{A/G}\), with \(\vec v_{A/G} = 60.0\,\hat\imath\ \text{km/h}\) and \(|\vec v_{P/A}| = 200\ \text{km/h}\) at an unknown angle \(\alpha\) west of north.

x components must cancel for due-north travel: \(-200\sin\alpha + 60.0 = 0 \Rightarrow \sin\alpha = 0.300 \Rightarrow \alpha = 17.5°\) west of north.

y component is the ground speed: \(v_{P/G} = 200\cos 17.5° = 191\ \text{km/h}\), due north.

Check: the ground speed is less than the airspeed, because part of the plane’s effort goes into cancelling the wind. ✓

Simulation: crossing a river

The river is 60 m wide. Watch from the shore, then switch to watching from the water: the boat simply moves along its heading while the shore slides past. The velocity triangle shows \(\vec v_{B/G} = \vec v_{B/W} + \vec v_{W/G}\).

Predict. With the boat pointing straight across, does a stronger current change the crossing time? Then find the heading that lands the boat directly opposite its start.

Optional: when Galileo’s rule breaks down

The rule \(v_{O/2} = v_{O/1} + v_{1/2}\) works perfectly at everyday speeds. Near the speed of light, it fails. This optional section shows why, and what replaces it (straight-line motion only).

A thought experiment with spaceships

A ship (frame 1) moves away from Earth (frame 2) at \(v_{1/2} = 0.60c\), where \(c = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}\) is the speed of light. An astronaut launches a probe forward at \(v_{O/1} = 0.70c\) relative to the ship.

Galileo’s rule predicts \(v_{O/2} = 0.70c + 0.60c = 1.30c\): faster than light. Worse, if the astronaut switches on a forward-pointing laser, Galileo predicts that the light moves at \(c + 0.60c = 1.60c\) relative to Earth.

That is not what happens. Every measurement agrees that light travels at the same speed \(c\) in every inertial frame, whatever the motion of its source. In 1964, for example, physicists at CERN timed gamma rays emitted by particles moving at 99.975% of \(c\). The gamma rays still travelled at \(c\), not at nearly \(2c\).

Einstein’s fix

Einstein (1905) took the constancy of \(c\) as a starting principle. The price is that time intervals and lengths are no longer the same for all observers. One consequence is a new rule for adding velocities along a line:

\[ v_{O/2} = \frac{v_{O/1} + v_{1/2}}{1 + \dfrac{v_{O/1}\,v_{1/2}}{c^2}} \]

Same subscripts, same chain: \(O/1\) then \(1/2\) gives \(O/2\). The velocities are signed (+ or −) along the line, exactly as in Section 4.3. Only the denominator is new.

The spaceship again

Probe: \(v_{O/2} = \dfrac{0.70c + 0.60c}{1 + (0.70)(0.60)} = \dfrac{1.30c}{1.42} = 0.915c\). Fast, but below \(c\).

Laser light (\(v_{O/1} = c\)): \(v_{O/2} = \dfrac{c + 0.60c}{1 + 0.60} = c\). Exactly \(c\), for any ship speed.

Show where the formula comes from

Let frame 1 move at \(v = v_{1/2}\) along x relative to frame 2. Special relativity relates the coordinates of an event in the two frames by the Lorentz transformation, with \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}\):

\[ x_1 = \gamma\,(x_2 - v\,t_2) \qquad t_1 = \gamma\left(t_2 - \frac{v\,x_2}{c^2}\right) \]

For a moving object, take small changes: \(dx_1 = \gamma\,(dx_2 - v\,dt_2)\) and \(dt_1 = \gamma\,(dt_2 - v\,dx_2/c^2)\). Divide, and write \(u_2 = dx_2/dt_2 = v_{O/2}\) and \(u_1 = dx_1/dt_1 = v_{O/1}\):

\[ u_1 = \frac{u_2 - v}{1 - u_2 v/c^2} \]

Solve for \(u_2\): \(u_2 = \dfrac{u_1 + v}{1 + u_1 v/c^2}\), which is the formula above. Notice that the time measured in frame 1 differs from the time in frame 2; with a shared clock (\(t_1 = t_2\)), you would get Galileo’s rule instead.

Galileo is right at low speeds

The two rules differ only through the term \(v_{O/1}v_{1/2}/c^2\) in the denominator. At everyday speeds it is tiny:

Size of the relativistic correction \(v_{O/1}v_{1/2}/c^2\)
Situation\(v_{O/1}\)\(v_{1/2}\)\(v_{O/1}v_{1/2}/c^2\)
Walking on a train1.5 m/s20 m/s3.3 × 10⁻¹⁶
Missile fired from a jet1000 m/s600 m/s6.7 × 10⁻¹²
Probe released from a spacecraft3 km/s17 km/s5.7 × 10⁻¹⁰
The ship and probe above0.70c0.60c0.42

When \(v_{O/1}v_{1/2} \ll c^2\), the denominator is 1 to many decimal places, and

\[ v_{O/2} \approx v_{O/1} + v_{1/2} \]

Galileo’s rule is the low-speed limit of Einstein’s. The theory we use every day is not wrong; it is an excellent approximation inside its range.

Big ideas to carry forward

  • Work one component at a time. Time is what links the components.
  • An object can accelerate without changing speed: turning is accelerating.
  • Velocity depends on the observer; acceleration does not (for inertial frames).
  • Galileo’s velocity addition is the everyday limit of Einstein’s.

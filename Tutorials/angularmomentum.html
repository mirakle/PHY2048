Angular momentum

Why does a skater spin faster when she pulls her arms in? Why does a spinning football fly nose-first while a wobbly one tumbles? One quantity answers both: angular momentum, the rotational partner of linear momentum.

Part 1

What is angular momentum?

After this part you can

  • describe angular momentum as the “amount of rotational motion” of an object about a point;
  • compute \(\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p\) for a particle, including one moving in a straight line;
  • compute \(L = I\omega\) for a rigid body turning about a fixed axis, and find its direction with the right-hand rule.

1.1 An intuitive picture

Linear momentum, \(\vec p = m\vec v\), measures how much “motion in a straight line” an object carries: a loaded truck at 30 m/s is much harder to stop than a bicycle at the same speed. Angular momentum plays the same role for turning.

Think of a spinning merry-go-round. It is harder to stop when

  • it is heavier (more mass),
  • it is spinning faster (larger \(\omega\)),
  • its mass is farther from the axis (larger radius).

Angular momentum combines all three. And just as a force is needed to change \(\vec p\), a torque is needed to change angular momentum (Part 2). With no torque, it stays fixed (Part 3).

Key idea

Angular momentum is always measured about a point (or an axis). Change the point and the angular momentum changes too, just as torque does.

1.2 A particle: \(\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p\)

For a particle of mass \(m\) moving with momentum \(\vec p = m\vec v\), with \(\vec r\) the position vector from the chosen point O to the particle, the angular momentum about O is

\[ \vec L = \vec r\times\vec p = m\,\vec r\times\vec v . \]

It has the same structure as torque, \(\vec\tau = \vec r\times\vec F\), with \(\vec F\) replaced by \(\vec p\). Its size can be read three equivalent ways:

\[ L = rp\sin\phi = r_\perp\,p = r\,p_\perp , \]

where \(\phi\) is the angle between \(\vec r\) and \(\vec p\), \(r_\perp\) is the perpendicular distance from O to the line along which the particle moves, and \(p_\perp\) is the part of \(\vec p\) perpendicular to \(\vec r\). Its direction follows the right-hand rule: fingers along \(\vec r\), curl toward \(\vec p\), thumb along \(\vec L\).

Common trap

“Only things that go around in circles have angular momentum.” A particle moving in a straight line has angular momentum about any point not on its line. And because \(r_\perp\) never changes along the line, if no force acts, \(L = mvr_\perp\) stays constant. The animation below shows this.

Example: a puck sliding past a point

A 0.50 kg puck slides at 4.0 m/s along a straight line that passes 1.5 m from a point O. About O, \(L = mvr_\perp = (0.50)(4.0)(1.5) = 3.0\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}\), at every moment of its motion.

Example: a particle in a circle

For a particle moving in a circle of radius \(r\) around O, \(\vec r\) and \(\vec v\) are always perpendicular, so \(L = mvr = mr^2\omega\). Earth orbiting the Sun (mass \(5.97\times10^{24}\ \text{kg}\), speed \(2.98\times10^{4}\ \text{m/s}\), radius \(1.50\times10^{11}\ \text{m}\)) has \(L \approx 2.7\times10^{40}\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}\).

1.4 A rigid body turning about a fixed axis: \(L = I\omega\)

A rigid body spinning about a fixed axis is made of particles moving in circles. Particle \(i\), at distance \(r_i\) from the axis, has \(L_i = m_i r_i^2\omega\), all pointing along the axis. Adding them up:

\[ L = \Big(\sum_i m_i r_i^2\Big)\omega = I\omega , \qquad \vec L = I\vec\omega . \]

The direction of \(\vec L\) is along the axis, given by the right-hand rule: curl the fingers of your right hand in the direction of rotation; your thumb points along \(\vec\omega\) and \(\vec L\).

Example: a record on a turntable

A vinyl record and platter act as a solid disk of mass 2.0 kg and radius 0.15 m turning at 33⅓ rpm (\(\omega = 3.49\ \text{rad/s}\)). Then \(I = \tfrac12(2.0)(0.15)^2 = 0.0225\ \text{kg·m}^2\) and \(L = I\omega = 0.079\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}\), pointing down if the record turns clockwise seen from above.

When is \(\vec L\) not parallel to \(\vec\omega\)? For information, not tested

\(\vec L = I\vec\omega\) holds when the body is symmetric about its rotation axis (a wheel, a disk, a sphere spinning about a symmetry axis). For a lopsided object, such as a wheel mounted crooked on its axle, \(\vec L\) is not parallel to \(\vec\omega\) and wobbles around it; the axle must then supply a changing torque, which is why unbalanced car wheels shake (Brown, sections 6.3 and 6.6).

Part 2

Torque changes angular momentum

After this part you can

  • state Newton’s second law for rotation as \(\vec\tau_\text{net} = d\vec L/dt\) and connect it to \(\tau = I\alpha\);
  • use the angular impulse \(\Delta L = \tau\,\Delta t\) for a constant torque;
  • solve spin-up and braking problems with a constant torque;
  • explain why a torque perpendicular to \(\vec L\) changes its direction, not its size.

2.1 Torque is the rate of change of angular momentum

For linear motion, Newton’s second law in its original form is \(\vec F_\text{net} = d\vec p/dt\): a net force changes momentum. The rotational version is

\[ \boxed{\ \vec\tau_\text{net} = \frac{d\vec L}{dt}\ } \]

where both the torque and the angular momentum are measured about the same point.

Show the derivation for a particle

Differentiate \(\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p\) with the product rule:

\[ \frac{d\vec L}{dt} = \frac{d\vec r}{dt}\times\vec p + \vec r\times\frac{d\vec p}{dt} = \vec v\times(m\vec v) + \vec r\times\vec F_\text{net} = \vec 0 + \vec\tau_\text{net} . \]

The first term vanishes because the cross product of a vector with itself is zero. For a system of particles, the internal forces come in equal and opposite pairs along the lines joining the particles, so their torques cancel and only the external torque remains (Brown, section 6.2).

For a rigid body turning about a fixed axis with constant \(I\), \(dL/dt = I\,d\omega/dt = I\alpha\), so the law gives back \(\tau_\text{net} = I\alpha\). But \(\tau = dL/dt\) is more general: it still works when \(I\) changes (a skater, Part 3) or when the direction of \(\vec L\) changes (a gyroscope, Part 4).

2.2 A constant torque

If a constant torque acts along the axis for a time \(\Delta t\), the angular momentum changes at a steady rate:

\[ L(t) = L_0 + \tau t, \qquad \Delta L = \tau\,\Delta t, \qquad \omega(t) = \frac{L_0 + \tau t}{I} . \]

A graph of \(L\) against \(t\) is a straight line of slope \(\tau\). The same torque for the same time gives the same \(\Delta L\) to any object; how fast it ends up spinning depends on its \(I\).

2.3 Worked examples

Example 1: pushing a merry-go-round

A playground merry-go-round is a solid disk, \(M = 150\ \text{kg}\), \(R = 2.0\ \text{m}\), at rest. A child pushes tangentially on the rim with 50 N for 4.0 s.

\(\tau = RF = (2.0)(50) = 100\ \text{N·m}\), so \(\Delta L = \tau\,\Delta t = (100)(4.0) = 400\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}\). With \(I = \tfrac12MR^2 = 300\ \text{kg·m}^2\), the final angular speed is \(\omega = L/I = 1.33\ \text{rad/s}\), about one turn every 4.7 s.

Example 2: spinning up a bicycle wheel

A bicycle wheel (hoop, \(M = 1.2\ \text{kg}\), \(R = 0.33\ \text{m}\)) is spun by hand with a steady torque of 1.5 N·m. How long to reach 20 rad/s? \(I = MR^2 = 0.131\ \text{kg·m}^2\), so the target is \(L = I\omega = 2.61\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}\), and \(\Delta t = \Delta L/\tau = 2.61/1.5 = 1.7\ \text{s}\).

Example 3: braking, revisited

The grinding wheel from the previous notes (\(I = 0.0080\ \text{kg·m}^2\), \(\omega_0 = 120\ \text{rad/s}\)) is slowed by a friction torque of 0.40 N·m. Its angular momentum is \(L_0 = 0.96\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}\), and a torque of \(-0.40\ \text{N·m}\) removes it in \(\Delta t = 0.96/0.40 = 2.4\ \text{s}\). (The energy method told us how many turns; the angular impulse tells us how long.)

Which tool?

Torque × time = change in angular momentum. Torque × angle = work = change in rotational kinetic energy. Pick the one that matches what the problem gives you.

2.5 A torque can change the direction of \(\vec L\)

\(\vec\tau = d\vec L/dt\) is a vector equation: in a short time, the torque adds \(d\vec L = \vec\tau\,dt\) to \(\vec L\).

  • A torque parallel to \(\vec L\) makes it longer: the object spins faster.
  • A torque antiparallel to \(\vec L\) makes it shorter: the object slows down.
  • A torque perpendicular to \(\vec L\) tilts it: its size stays the same, but its direction turns. This is the key to gyroscopes and spinning tops (Part 4).

Part 3

Conservation of angular momentum

After this part you can

  • state when angular momentum is conserved;
  • use \(I_i\omega_i = I_f\omega_f\) for skaters, rotating stools and colliding disks;
  • explain why rotational kinetic energy changes even when \(L\) is conserved;
  • treat \(\vec L\) as a vector to explain the bicycle-wheel flip.

3.1 The conservation law

If the net external torque on a system is zero, \(d\vec L/dt = \vec 0\):

\[ \boxed{\ \vec\tau_\text{ext} = \vec 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec L_\text{total} = \text{constant}\ } \]

Internal forces, however strong, cannot change the total angular momentum: you cannot spin yourself up by pulling on your own arms. They can, however, move angular momentum from one part of a system to another, or change the system’s moment of inertia.

The law can also hold for one component only. If the external torques have no vertical component (for example, a frictionless stool that can only turn about a vertical axis), then \(L_z\) is conserved even if the other components are not.

For a system turning about a fixed axis, conservation of \(L = I\omega\) reads

\[ I_i\,\omega_i = I_f\,\omega_f . \]

3.2 The ice skater

Ice is nearly frictionless, so a spinning skater feels almost no torque about her vertical axis. With her arms out, \(I_i = 3.5\ \text{kg·m}^2\) and she spins at \(\omega_i = 2.0\ \text{rad/s}\). She pulls her arms in to \(I_f = 1.0\ \text{kg·m}^2\):

\[ \omega_f = \frac{I_i}{I_f}\,\omega_i = \frac{3.5}{1.0}(2.0) = 7.0\ \text{rad/s}. \]

Her kinetic energy goes from \(\tfrac12(3.5)(2.0)^2 = 7.0\ \text{J}\) to \(\tfrac12(1.0)(7.0)^2 = 24.5\ \text{J}\). The extra 17.5 J is work done by her muscles pulling her arms inward against their tendency to fly outward.

Common trap

“If angular momentum is conserved, so is kinetic energy.” No: with \(L\) fixed, \(K_\text{rot} = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 = \dfrac{L^2}{2I}\), which increases when \(I\) decreases. The two conservation laws are independent.

3.4 Rotational collisions

A turntable disk (\(I_1 = 0.020\ \text{kg·m}^2\)) spins at 10 rad/s. An identical disk, not rotating, is dropped onto it, and the two end up turning together. During the short collision the axle exerts no torque about the axis, so

\[ I_1\omega_1 = (I_1 + I_2)\,\omega_f \quad\Rightarrow\quad \omega_f = \frac{0.020}{0.040}(10) = 5.0\ \text{rad/s}. \]

Kinetic energy drops from 1.0 J to 0.50 J: friction between the disks turns half of it into heat. This is the rotational version of a perfectly inelastic collision.

3.5 The bicycle-wheel flip

A student sits at rest on a stool that turns freely about a vertical axis. She holds a bicycle wheel spinning with its axle vertical, so the wheel’s angular momentum \(\vec L_w\) points up. Then she flips the wheel upside down. Nobody pushes her, yet she starts to turn, in the direction opposite to the wheel’s new spin.

Why? Only vertical torques could change the vertical angular momentum of the system (student + stool + wheel), and the frictionless stool exerts none. So \(L_z\) is conserved:

\[ \underbrace{+L_w}_{\text{before}} = \underbrace{-L_w}_{\text{wheel after}} + L_\text{student} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad L_\text{student} = +2L_w . \]

To flip the wheel, she must exert a torque on it; by Newton’s third law the wheel exerts an equal and opposite torque on her, which sets her (and the stool) turning.

Part 4

Spin and stability

After this part you can

  • explain why a large angular momentum makes an object’s orientation hard to change;
  • estimate how much a brief disturbing torque tilts a spinning object;
  • describe gyroscopic precession and compute its rate;
  • discuss what does, and does not, keep a moving bicycle upright.

4.1 Why spin stabilizes

Suppose a brief disturbance (a gust, a bump, a fingertip) gives an object an angular impulse \(\Delta\vec L\) perpendicular to its spin axis.

  • Not spinning: \(\vec L\) was zero, so afterwards \(\vec L = \Delta\vec L\) and the object starts to tumble, turning end over end at \(\omega = \Delta L/I\) for as long as it flies.
  • Spinning fast: \(\vec L_\text{spin}\) is large. Adding a small perpendicular \(\Delta\vec L\) tilts the total by only a small angle, \[ \theta \approx \frac{\Delta L}{L_\text{spin}} \ \ (\text{radians}), \] and the spin axis just wobbles around this slightly tilted direction.
Key idea

The same disturbance that would send a non-spinning object tumbling only tilts a fast-spinning one slightly. The larger \(L\), the smaller the tilt. This is spin stabilization, used by rifled bullets, thrown discs, spacecraft and footballs.

4.2 A well-thrown football

A spiral pass spins at roughly 600 rpm (63 rad/s) about the ball’s long axis. With a moment of inertia about that axis of about \(0.0020\ \text{kg·m}^2\), \(L_\text{spin} \approx 0.13\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}\). A gust delivering an angular impulse of \(0.010\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}\) tilts the axis by only \(\theta \approx 0.010/0.13 = 0.08\ \text{rad} \approx 4.5^\circ\). Thrown without spin, the same gust would set it tumbling at about \(0.010/0.0035 \approx 3\ \text{rad/s}\), half a turn per second.

The small wobble you see in a good spiral is this motion: the long axis circling around the direction of \(\vec L\). Why the nose also turns gently downward to follow the curved path is a subtler aerodynamic effect (Brancazio 1987; Price and Moss 2020).

4.4 The gyroscope: a steady torque perpendicular to \(\vec L\)

Hold a spinning bicycle wheel by one end of its axle, with the axle horizontal, and let go of the other end. Instead of falling, the wheel’s axle swings slowly around the support in a horizontal circle. This is precession.

The torque of gravity about the pivot has size \(\tau = Mgd\) and is always horizontal and perpendicular to \(\vec L\). In a time \(dt\), \(\vec L\) turns through a small angle \(d\phi = \tau\,dt/L\). The precession rate is

\[ \omega_p = \frac{d\phi}{dt} = \frac{\tau}{L} = \frac{Mgd}{I\omega} . \]

Example. A bicycle wheel (\(M = 2.0\ \text{kg}\), \(I = 0.18\ \text{kg·m}^2\)) spins at 20 rad/s with its center 0.20 m from the pivot: \(\omega_p = (2.0)(9.8)(0.20)/((0.18)(20)) = 1.1\ \text{rad/s}\), about one turn every 6 s. Spin it twice as fast and it precesses half as fast. Slow it down and the precession speeds up, and the wheel eventually droops and falls.

A spinning top For information, not tested

A top tilted at angle \(\theta\) to the vertical, with its center of mass a distance \(D\) from the tip, feels a gravitational torque \(MgD\sin\theta\). Its angular momentum has a horizontal component \(L\sin\theta\), which swings around at \(\omega_p\), so \(MgD\sin\theta = L\sin\theta\,\omega_p\) and again \(\omega_p = MgD/L\), independent of the tilt (Brown, section 6.7).

A spinning bicycle wheel shows precession, caused by the torque of its weight. The big idea: a torque adds angular momentum in the direction of the torque. If there is no angular momentum to begin with, the wheel swings (falls) so that it rotates in the direction of the torque; if it is already spinning, the torque changes the direction of its angular momentum instead, and the wheel precesses.

4.5 Why doesn’t a moving bicycle fall over?

It is tempting to say: “the spinning wheels act like gyroscopes.” The wheels do have angular momentum, and if the bike starts to lean, the torque of gravity makes the front wheel precess, steering it into the lean, which brings the wheels back under the bike. That effect is real, and it helps.

But it is not the whole story. In 2011, researchers built a bicycle with extra counter-rotating wheels that cancel the wheels’ angular momentum, and with a steering geometry that removes the usual “caster” trail. Pushed sideways while rolling, it still steered itself upright (Kooijman et al., Science 2011). A moving bicycle stays up mainly because leaning makes it steer into the lean, through a combination of gyroscopic effects, the steering geometry and how mass is distributed in the frame and fork. Above a certain speed, these effects together make it self-stable; a rider adds active steering on top.

Wrap-up

Summary: the main points

1. What is angular momentum?

  • Angular momentum measures the amount of rotational motion about a point; it grows with mass, speed and distance from the point.
  • For a particle, \(\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p\), with size \(L = r_\perp p\). A particle moving in a straight line has constant angular momentum about any point, if no force acts.
  • For a rigid body turning about a symmetry axis, \(\vec L = I\vec\omega\), along the axis by the right-hand rule. Units: kg·m²/s.

2. Torque changes angular momentum

  • \(\vec\tau_\text{net} = d\vec L/dt\), the rotational form of \(\vec F = d\vec p/dt\). For constant \(I\) it reduces to \(\tau = I\alpha\).
  • A constant torque gives \(\Delta L = \tau\,\Delta t\) (angular impulse): the same to every object, while \(\Delta\omega = \tau\,\Delta t/I\) depends on \(I\).
  • Torque × time changes \(L\); torque × angle (work) changes \(K_\text{rot}\).
  • A torque parallel to \(\vec L\) changes its size; a torque perpendicular to \(\vec L\) changes its direction.

3. Conservation of angular momentum

  • If the net external torque is zero, the total \(\vec L\) is constant; if only one component of the torque is zero, that component of \(\vec L\) is conserved.
  • About a fixed axis: \(I_i\omega_i = I_f\omega_f\). Pulling mass inward (skater, stool with dumbbells) makes the system spin faster.
  • Kinetic energy is not conserved along with \(L\): \(K = L^2/2I\) increases when \(I\) decreases (work done by muscles) and decreases in a rotational collision (heat).
  • \(\vec L\) is a vector: flipping a spinning wheel from \(+L_w\) to \(-L_w\) gives the student and stool \(+2L_w\).

4. Spin and stability

  • A brief disturbance \(\Delta L\) makes a non-spinning object tumble, but only tilts a spinning one by about \(\Delta L/L_\text{spin}\): spin stabilization (footballs, bullets, spacecraft).
  • A steady torque perpendicular to \(\vec L\) makes it precess: \(\omega_p = \tau/L = Mgd/(I\omega)\); faster spin, slower precession.
  • A moving bicycle stays upright mainly because leaning makes it steer into the lean; the wheels’ angular momentum helps, but a bicycle can be self-stable without it.

Common traps

  • Thinking only circular motion has angular momentum.
  • Mixing reference points: torque and angular momentum must be taken about the same point.
  • Assuming kinetic energy is conserved whenever angular momentum is.
  • Treating \(L\) as a number when its direction matters (the bicycle-wheel flip, precession).
  • Thinking internal forces can change a system’s total angular momentum.

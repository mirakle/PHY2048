Test 3 Study Guide

Work and energy, linear momentum, rotational kinematics and dynamics, rotational kinetic energy and rolling, and angular momentum. Every section has the key ideas, the equations, the mistakes that cost the most points, a visual tool to explore, and checkpoints to test yourself.

How to use this guide

  1. Read the key ideas of a section. Equations in orange boxes are the ones to know.
  2. Play with the visual tool. Predict what will happen before you move a slider, then check. Every tool also reports its result in words.
  3. Do the checkpoints. Pick an answer and select Check. You get feedback and the reasoning either way. Your score for each section appears in the contents.
  4. Miss one? Re-read the matching idea, then try a full problem in the Test 3 Online Practice.

Use \(g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\) throughout. Counterclockwise is positive for rotation. You will not be asked to compute integrals for a center of mass or a moment of inertia: use the table of moments of inertia.

The big picture: every linear idea has a rotational twin

Test 3 is really two stories told twice. Energy and momentum for an object moving along a line, then the same two ideas for an object turning about an axis. Learn the left column and you already know the right column: swap \(x \to \theta\), \(v \to \omega\), \(a \to \alpha\), \(m \to I\), \(F \to \tau\), \(p \to L\).

1a. Work done by a force

Work is a scalar (joules). Only the part of the force along the displacement does work. Pick the method that matches what the problem gives you:

Choosing a method for work
The problem gives…Use
Magnitudes and the angle \(\phi\) between \(\vec F\) and \(\Delta\vec r\)\(W = F\,d\cos\phi\)
Components (\(\hat\imath,\hat\jmath\) form)\(W = F_x\Delta x + F_y\Delta y\)
A formula \(F(x)\)\(W = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} F(x)\,dx\)
A graph of \(F\) versus \(x\)signed area under the curve (below the axis counts negative)

Positive work speeds an object up, negative work slows it down, and a force perpendicular to the motion (a normal force on a level floor, a vertical push on a sliding box) does zero work.

The sign of work

A box slides to the right through a displacement \(d\). Change the angle between the force and the displacement and watch the component that does the work.

1b. Kinetic energy and the work–energy theorem

\[K = \tfrac12 mv^2 \qquad\qquad W_\text{net} = K_f - K_i\]

The net work (all forces together) equals the change in kinetic energy. \(K\) is never negative and grows with the square of the speed: doubling the speed quadruples \(K\).

Pushed over a fixed distance? Think work and energy. Pushed for a fixed time? Think impulse and momentum (section 2).

Traps

  • Forgetting the initial kinetic energy when the object is already moving.
  • Adding work to a speed. Work changes \(K\); solve for \(v_f = \sqrt{v_i^2 + 2W_\text{net}/m}\) and keep the square root.

1c. Potential energy, friction and conservation of energy

A conservative force (gravity, a spring) stores energy you can get back: \(\Delta U = -W_c\).

\[U_g = mgy \qquad U_e = \tfrac12 kx^2 \qquad \Delta E_\text{th} = f_k d = \mu_k F_N d\]

Write the energy bar chart as an equation. Both sides must balance:

\[K_i + U_{gi} + U_{ei} + W_\text{ext} = K_f + U_{gf} + U_{ef} + \Delta E_\text{th}\]
  1. Choose the system, the initial state and the final state.
  2. Decide which bars are zero (starts from rest? spring relaxed? chosen \(y = 0\)?).
  3. On an incline, \(F_N = mg\cos\theta\) and the sliding distance is \(d = h/\sin\theta\), not \(h\).

With no friction, the speed after dropping a height \(h\) from rest is \(\sqrt{2gh}\), whatever the path or the mass. Power is the rate of doing work: \(P = dW/dt = Fv\), in watts.

Traps

  • Using the height of the incline as the friction distance.
  • Dropping the ½ in \(\tfrac12 kx^2\).
  • Counting gravity twice: use either \(W_\text{gravity}\) or \(U_g\), never both.

Energy bar chart: a block sliding down an incline

The block starts at the top with speed \(v_0\) and slides down. Kinetic friction acts the whole way. Watch how the initial energy is shared at the end.

2a. Momentum and impulse

\[\vec p = m\vec v \qquad\qquad \vec J = \Delta\vec p = \int \vec F\,dt = \vec F_\text{avg}\,\Delta t\]
  • Momentum is a vector: keep the sign of every velocity.
  • Impulse is the signed area under an F–t graph, not its height.
  • The same net force for the same time gives the same \(\Delta p\), whatever the mass. (The light object ends up faster, but with the same momentum change.)
  • A bounce gives a bigger \(\Delta p\) than a stop: \(\Delta p = m(v_f - v_i)\) with \(v_f\) of opposite sign.
  • Newton's second law in its general form: \(\sum\vec F = d\vec p/dt\).

Text description: the impulse is the area between the force curve and the time axis. The average force is the height of a rectangle with the same area and the same duration \(\Delta t\), so \(J = F_\text{avg}\Delta t\).

2b. Collisions and conservation of momentum

For an isolated system (no net external force), the total momentum is the same before and after any collision or explosion:

\[m_1v_{1i} + m_2v_{2i} = m_1v_{1f} + m_2v_{2f}\]

The second equation describes the bounce, with the coefficient of restitution \(r\):

\[v_{2f} - v_{1f} = r\,(v_{1i} - v_{2i})\]
Types of collisions
Type\(r\)MomentumKinetic energy
Elastic1conservedconserved
Partially inelasticbetween 0 and 1conservedsome lost to heat and sound
Perfectly inelastic (stick)0conservedmaximum loss; \(v_f = \dfrac{m_1v_{1i}+m_2v_{2i}}{m_1+m_2}\)

The two objects receive equal and opposite impulses (Newton's third law). Total energy is always conserved; only kinetic energy can be lost.

Traps

  • Writing the restitution equation with the velocities in the wrong order.
  • Forgetting the minus sign of an object moving left.
  • Assuming kinetic energy is conserved in every collision.

3a. Describing rotation

Angle \(\theta\) in radians, angular velocity \(\omega = d\theta/dt\), angular acceleration \(\alpha = d\omega/dt\). With constant \(\alpha\), the equations are the linear ones with new letters:

\[\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t \qquad \theta = \omega_0 t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2 \qquad \omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\,\Delta\theta\]

A point at distance \(r\) from the axis has speed \(v = r\omega\), a tangential acceleration \(a_t = r\alpha\) (speeding up or slowing down) and a centripetal acceleration \(a_c = \omega^2 r\) (pointing to the axis). These are different: a wheel just starting from rest has \(a_t \ne 0\) but \(a_c = 0\).

Conversions: \(1\ \text{rev} = 2\pi\ \text{rad}\); \(\text{rpm} \times 2\pi/60 = \text{rad/s}\).

3b. Center of mass

\[x_\text{cm} = \frac{\sum m_i x_i}{M} \qquad y_\text{cm} = \frac{\sum m_i y_i}{M}\]

A mass-weighted average: the center of mass sits closer to the heavier parts. For a uniform plate, split it into rectangles and weight each one by its area; for thin rods, weight by length. A symmetric uniform object has its center of mass on its line of symmetry.

3c. Torque and static equilibrium

\[\tau = rF\sin\theta = (\text{lever arm})\times F \qquad \vec\tau = \vec r\times\vec F\]

\(r\) is measured from the pivot to where the force acts; \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\vec r\) and \(\vec F\). The lever arm \(r\sin\theta\) is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action. Counterclockwise is positive. Decide each sign from the way that force alone would turn the object.

Static equilibrium: \(\sum F = 0\) and \(\sum\tau = 0\) about any point. A uniform beam's weight acts at its center. Choose the pivot at an unknown force to remove it from the torque equation.

Torque on a bar

A 1 m bar is pinned at its left end. Move where the force acts, its size and its direction. The dashed line is the line of action; the dotted segment is the lever arm.

3d. Moment of inertia and Newton's second law for rotation

\[\sum\tau = I\alpha \qquad I = \sum m_i r_i^2 \qquad I = I_\text{cm} + Md^2\]

\(I\) measures how hard it is to change the rotation. It depends on the mass and on where the mass is relative to the axis. The parallel-axis theorem moves the axis a distance \(d\) away from the center of mass, and \(I\) always gets bigger.

Solid sphere\(\tfrac25 MR^2\)2/5
Hollow (thin-walled) sphere, like a basketball\(\tfrac23 MR^2\)2/3

Check the parallel-axis theorem yourself

Rod about its end: \(I_\text{cm} + M(L/2)^2 = \tfrac1{12}ML^2 + \tfrac14 ML^2 = \tfrac13 ML^2\). ✓

Traps

  • Using \(I\) about the center when the rod is pinned at its end.
  • Measuring distances from the end of the stick instead of from the pivot.
  • Forgetting the beam's own weight at its center.
  • Using rpm or revolutions in a formula that needs rad/s or radians; or leaving radians when the question asks for rotations.

Section 4Rotational kinetic energy and rolling motion

Covered in: Rigid Bodies Part 2, Rotational Kinetic Energy and Rolling.

Read in Knight, 5th ed.

  • §12.3 Rotational energy
  • §12.9 Rolling motion

4a. Rotational kinetic energy and work by a torque

\[K_\text{rot} = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 \qquad W = \tau\,\Delta\theta = \Delta\big(\tfrac12 I\omega^2\big) \qquad P = \tau\omega\]

\(\Delta\theta\) must be in radians. A braking torque does negative work and removes rotational kinetic energy.

Ranking spinning shapes depends on what is held equal. Same \(M\), \(R\) and \(\omega\): the hoop has the most \(K_\text{rot}\) (largest \(c\)). Same angular momentum: the hoop has the least, since \(K = L^2/2I\).

4b. Rolling without slipping

\[v_\text{cm} = R\omega \qquad a_\text{cm} = R\alpha \qquad K = \tfrac12 Mv_\text{cm}^2 + \tfrac12 I_\text{cm}\omega^2 = \tfrac12 Mv_\text{cm}^2\,(1+c)\]

A rolling object moves and spins. The fraction of its kinetic energy that is rotational is \(c/(1+c)\): half for a hoop, one-third for a solid cylinder, 2/7 for a solid sphere.

Rolling down a height \(h\) from rest (energy is conserved: static friction does no work because the contact point does not slide):

\[v = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1+c}} \qquad a = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1+c}\]

Mass and radius cancel. Only the shape factor \(c\) matters, so the race order is always: solid sphere (2/5), solid cylinder (1/2), hollow sphere (2/3), hoop (1). A frictionless sliding block (\(c = 0\)) would beat them all.

Rolling uphill from speed \(v_0\): the rotational energy also turns into height, so \(h = v_0^2(1+c)/2g\). At the same starting speed the hoop climbs highest.

Traps

  • Forgetting the rotational term, which gives the sliding-block answer \(\sqrt{2gh}\).
  • Using the wrong \(c\) for the shape.
  • Thinking heavier or bigger objects roll down faster.
  • Thinking static friction does work on an object rolling without slipping.

Section 5Angular momentum

Covered in: Rigid Bodies Part 3, Angular Momentum.

Read in Knight, 5th ed.

  • §12.10 The vector description of rotational motion (cross product, torque as a vector)
  • §12.11 Angular momentum
  • §12.12 Advanced topic: precession of a gyroscope (optional)

5a. What angular momentum is

\[\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p \ \ (\text{particle}),\quad L = mvr_\perp \qquad\qquad L = I\omega \ \ (\text{rigid body, fixed axis})\]

A particle moving in a straight line has angular momentum about any point not on its line: \(r_\perp\) is the perpendicular distance from the point to the line of motion. For a spinning body, \(\vec L\) points along the axis, by the right-hand rule: curl your fingers the way it turns, and your thumb gives \(\vec L\).

5b. Torque changes angular momentum

\[\sum\vec\tau = \frac{d\vec L}{dt} \qquad \Delta L = \tau\,\Delta t \ \ (\text{constant torque})\]

The rotational version of impulse. Torque and angular momentum must be taken about the same point. A torque perpendicular to \(\vec L\) changes its direction, not its size (a gyroscope precesses).

5c. Conservation of angular momentum

With no external torque, \(\vec L\) is constant. Internal forces cannot change it.

\[I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2\]
  • Changing shape (skater pulls in her arms, diver tucks): \(I\) drops, \(\omega\) rises. Kinetic energy increases, because \(K = L^2/2I\) and her muscles do work.
  • Rotational collision (clay sticks to a turntable, a ring drops on a spinning disk): add the moments of inertia, \(\omega_f = I_1\omega_1/(I_1 + I_2)\). Kinetic energy decreases, like a perfectly inelastic collision.
  • Vectors matter: a person on a stool who flips a spinning bicycle wheel starts turning the other way, so that the total \(\vec L\) stays the same.

Traps

  • Pointing \(\vec L\) along the motion of the rim instead of along the axis.
  • Assuming kinetic energy is conserved whenever angular momentum is.
  • Treating \(L\) as a number when its direction matters (the wheel flip).
  • Thinking only circular motion has angular momentum.

Which principle do I use?

Read the question for what is given and what is asked. These questions, in order, settle most Test 3 problems.

  1. Collision, explosion, or a short hit? Use momentum: total \(p\) is conserved for the system, or \(J = \Delta p\) for one object. If something spins about a fixed axis, use \(L = I\omega\) conserved.
  2. Speeds at two positions or heights? Use energy with a bar chart. Include \(\tfrac12 I\omega^2\) if anything rolls or spins, and \(\Delta E_\text{th}\) if there is friction.
  3. An acceleration, a time, or a force? Use \(\sum F = ma\) and \(\sum\tau = I\alpha\), then the kinematics equations.
  4. Nothing moves? Static equilibrium: \(\sum F = 0\) and \(\sum\tau = 0\).

Collisions with a drop or a swing (two stages)

A bullet into a pendulum, a ball rolling down then hitting a block: use energy for the smooth part, momentum for the instant of the collision, then energy again. Never use energy conservation across an inelastic collision.

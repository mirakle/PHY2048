The rolling factor \(c\)

For round objects it is handy to write \(I = c\,MR^2\), where the number \(c = I/(MR^2)\) depends only on the shape. The more mass sits near the rim, the closer \(c\) is to 1. This one number decides how a round object shares its kinetic energy between translation and rotation when it rolls (Part 3).

Reminder: parallel-axis theorem

For an axis parallel to one through the center of mass, a distance \(d\) away: \(I = I_\text{cm} + Md^2\). We use it in Part 3 to see a rolling object as one turning about its contact point.

Rotational kinetic energy and rolling

A spinning wheel stores energy even if it goes nowhere. These notes find out how much, connect it to the work done by a torque, and then put translation and rotation together for objects that roll.

For instructors: how physics education research shaped these notes
  • Energy split in rolling. Students often assume the heavier or larger object wins a rolling race, or that all round objects tie. Part 1

Rotational kinetic energy

After this part you can

  • explain why a spinning object has kinetic energy even when its center of mass is at rest;
  • derive \(K_\text{rot} = \tfrac12 I\omega^2\) from \(\tfrac12 mv^2\);
  • compute the rotational kinetic energy of hoops, cylinders and spheres;
  • rank shapes by kinetic energy or by angular speed in different scenarios.

1.1 Reminder: kinetic energy of a particle

A particle of mass \(m\) moving at speed \(v\) has kinetic energy

\[ K = \tfrac12 mv^2 . \]
  • It is a scalar: it depends on the speed, not on the direction of motion, and it is never negative.
  • Its unit is the joule: \(1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}^2\).
  • For a system of particles, the kinetic energies simply add: \(K = \sum_i \tfrac12 m_i v_i^2\).

Example. A 1500 kg car at 20 m/s has \(K = \tfrac12(1500)(20)^2 = 3.0\times10^5\ \text{J}\). Double the speed and \(K\) is four times larger.

1.2 A spinning object has kinetic energy

A spinning wheel with its axle fixed goes nowhere: its center of mass is at rest, so \(\tfrac12 Mv_\text{cm}^2 = 0\). Yet every piece of the wheel is moving. Each piece is a particle, so each has kinetic energy, and the total is not zero.

Add up the kinetic energies of all the pieces. Piece \(i\), at distance \(r_i\) from the axis, moves at \(v_i = r_i\omega\):

\[ K_\text{rot} = \sum_i \tfrac12 m_i v_i^2 = \sum_i \tfrac12 m_i r_i^2\omega^2 = \tfrac12\Big(\sum_i m_i r_i^2\Big)\omega^2 . \]

\(\omega\) is the same for every piece, so it comes out of the sum, and what is left in the brackets is the moment of inertia:

\[ \boxed{\ K_\text{rot} = \tfrac12 I\omega^2\ } \qquad (\omega \text{ in rad/s}). \]
The same pattern as before: \(m \to I\), \(v \to \omega\)
TranslationRotation about a fixed axis
\(K = \tfrac12 mv^2\)\(K_\text{rot} = \tfrac12 I\omega^2\)
\(F_\text{net} = ma\)\(\tau_\text{net} = I\alpha\)
Key idea

Rotational kinetic energy is not a new kind of energy. It is the ordinary kinetic energy of all the pieces, added up; \(\tfrac12 I\omega^2\) is a shortcut for that sum.

Example: a dumbbell

Two 0.50 kg balls sit at the ends of a light 1.0 m rod spinning at 4.0 rad/s about its center. Then \(I = 2(0.50)(0.50)^2 = 0.25\ \text{kg·m}^2\) and \(K_\text{rot} = \tfrac12(0.25)(4.0)^2 = 2.0\ \text{J}\).

Explore: the dumbbell

Predict. If you double the length of the rod, by what factor does the kinetic energy change? What if you double \(\omega\) instead? What if only one ball is made heavier?

Check by adding particle energies: each ball moves at \(v = r\omega = (0.50)(4.0) = 2.0\ \text{m/s}\), so each has \(\tfrac12(0.50)(2.0)^2 = 1.0\ \text{J}\). Two balls: 2.0 J. Same answer.

Common trap

Use \(\omega\) in rad/s. A wheel at 3000 rpm has \(\omega = 3000 \times \tfrac{2\pi\ \text{rad}}{60\ \text{s}} = 314\ \text{rad/s}\), not 3000 or 50.

1.3 Hoops, cylinders and spheres

For round objects of mass \(M\) and radius \(R\), the table on the Moments of inertia tab gives \(I = c\,MR^2\), so

\[ K_\text{rot} = \tfrac12\,c\,MR^2\omega^2 = c \times \tfrac12 M(R\omega)^2 . \]

\(\tfrac12 M(R\omega)^2\) is the energy the object would have if all its mass moved at the rim speed \(R\omega\). The factor \(c\) says what fraction of that it really has:

hollow sphere (thin shell)\(\tfrac23MR^2\)0.67all mass at distance \(R\) from the center, but less from the axis near the poles

Example: a flywheel

A flywheel is a solid steel cylinder, \(M = 20\ \text{kg}\), \(R = 0.30\ \text{m}\), spinning at 3000 rpm (\(\omega = 314\ \text{rad/s}\)). Then \(I = \tfrac12(20)(0.30)^2 = 0.90\ \text{kg·m}^2\) and

\[ K_\text{rot} = \tfrac12(0.90)(314)^2 = 4.4\times10^4\ \text{J}, \]

about the kinetic energy of a 1500 kg car at 7.7 m/s, stored in an object that is not going anywhere. Flywheels are used to store energy in exactly this way.

Predict. Keep \(M\), \(R\) and \(\omega\) fixed and switch between the four shapes. Which one has the most kinetic energy? Which has the least? Then double \(\omega\): by what factor does \(K\) change?

1.5 Ranking shapes: it depends on what is held equal

Comparing shapes only makes sense once you say what they have in common. Three common scenarios:

  • Same \(M\), same \(R\), same \(\omega\). Then \(K_\text{rot} \propto c\): hoop \(>\) hollow sphere \(>\) solid cylinder \(>\) solid sphere.
  • Same \(M\), same \(\omega\), different sizes. Then \(K_\text{rot} \propto cR^2\). Size can beat shape: a solid sphere twice as wide as a hoop has \(0.40 \times 2^2 = 1.6\) times the hoop’s energy.
  • Same \(K_\text{rot}\), same \(M\) and \(R\). Then \(\omega = \sqrt{2K/(cMR^2)}\): the shape with the smallest \(c\) (the solid sphere) spins fastest.
Try it

Set up each scenario in the simulation, then tick Compare all four shapes to check your ranking.

Part 2

The work–energy theorem, linear and rotational

After this part you can

  • state the work–kinetic energy theorem for a particle;
  • compute the work done by a constant torque, \(W = \tau\,\Delta\theta\);
  • use \(W_\text{net} = \Delta K_\text{rot}\) to find angular speeds without time;
  • connect torque, angle, work, power and rotational kinetic energy.

2.1 Reminder: the work–energy theorem for a particle

A constant force \(\vec F\) acting on a particle that moves through a displacement \(\Delta\vec r\) does work

\[ W = \vec F\cdot\Delta\vec r = F\,\Delta r\cos\phi . \]

Only the part of the force along the motion does work. The work–kinetic energy theorem says that the net work done on the particle equals the change in its kinetic energy:

\[ W_\text{net} = \Delta K = \tfrac12 mv_f^2 - \tfrac12 mv_i^2 . \]

Example. A net force of 10 N pushes a 2.0 kg block from rest through 3.0 m. Then \(W_\text{net} = (10)(3.0) = 30\ \text{J} = \tfrac12(2.0)v_f^2\), so \(v_f = 5.5\ \text{m/s}\), found without knowing the time.

2.2 Work done by a torque

Push tangentially on the rim of a wheel of radius \(r\) with a force \(F_\perp\). As the wheel turns through a small angle \(d\theta\), the point where you push moves along an arc \(ds = r\,d\theta\), in the direction of the force. The work you do is

\[ dW = F_\perp\,ds = F_\perp\,r\,d\theta = \tau\,d\theta . \]

For a constant torque turning an object through an angle \(\Delta\theta\) (in radians),

\[ \boxed{\ W = \tau\,\Delta\theta\ } \]

just as \(W = F\,\Delta x\) for a constant force. A force component along the radius (through the axis) has no torque and does no rotational work.

The rotational work–energy theorem

\[ \boxed{\ W_\text{net} = \Delta K_\text{rot} = \tfrac12 I\omega_f^2 - \tfrac12 I\omega_i^2\ } \]
Show the derivation

Start from \(\tau_\text{net} = I\alpha\) and use the chain rule: \(\alpha = \dfrac{d\omega}{dt} = \dfrac{d\omega}{d\theta}\dfrac{d\theta}{dt} = \omega\dfrac{d\omega}{d\theta}\). Then

\[ W_\text{net} = \int \tau_\text{net}\,d\theta = \int I\omega\,\frac{d\omega}{d\theta}\,d\theta = \int_{\omega_i}^{\omega_f} I\omega\,d\omega = \tfrac12 I\omega_f^2 - \tfrac12 I\omega_i^2 . \]

This is the same calculation that turns \(F = ma\) into the work–energy theorem for a particle, with \(m \to I\), \(v \to \omega\), \(x \to \theta\).

2.3 Tying torque and energy together: the bar, again

In the guided exercise a 4.0 N force, always perpendicular, acts at the end of a bar (\(L = 1.2\ \text{m}\), \(M = 2.4\ \text{kg}\)) pivoted at the other end, for 3.0 s from rest. With torques we found \(|\tau| = 4.8\ \text{N·m}\), \(I = \tfrac13ML^2 = 1.152\ \text{kg·m}^2\), \(|\alpha| = 4.17\ \text{rad/s}^2\), an angle of \(18.75\ \text{rad}\) and a final \(|\omega| = 12.5\ \text{rad/s}\). Now look at energy two ways.

Route 1: work done by the torque

\(W = \tau\,\Delta\theta = (4.8)(18.75) = 90\ \text{J}\).

Route 2: kinetic energy at the end

\(K_\text{rot} = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 = \tfrac12(1.152)(12.5)^2 = 90\ \text{J}\).

They agree, as the theorem says they must: all the work done by the torque went into rotational kinetic energy. The energy route also answers a question without time: “how fast is the bar turning after 3 full turns?” \(W = (4.8)(3 \times 2\pi) = 90.5\ \text{J}\), so \(\omega = \sqrt{2(90.5)/1.152} = 12.5\ \text{rad/s}\).

Key idea

Torque and time give \(\alpha\) and \(\omega(t)\) (Newton’s second law). Torque and angle give the work, and so the kinetic energy (work–energy theorem). Use whichever matches the information you have.

2.4 Negative work: braking a grinding wheel

A grinding wheel (solid disk, \(M = 1.6\ \text{kg}\), \(R = 0.10\ \text{m}\)) spins at 120 rad/s. When the motor is switched off, friction in the bearings exerts a constant torque of 0.40 N·m against the rotation. How many turns does it make before stopping?

\(I = \tfrac12(1.6)(0.10)^2 = 0.0080\ \text{kg·m}^2\), so \(K_i = \tfrac12(0.0080)(120)^2 = 57.6\ \text{J}\). The friction torque does negative work \(W = -\tau\,\Delta\theta\) until \(K = 0\):

\[ -(0.40)\,\Delta\theta = 0 - 57.6 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta\theta = 144\ \text{rad} = 22.9\ \text{rev}. \]

The 57.6 J does not vanish: it heats the bearings.

2.5 Rotational power

Dividing \(dW = \tau\,d\theta\) by \(dt\) gives the rate at which a torque does work: \(P = \tau\omega\). An electric motor delivering 0.80 N·m at 300 rad/s supplies \(P = (0.80)(300) = 240\ \text{W}\). For a car engine this is why both torque and rotation rate matter.

Part 3

Rolling motion

After this part you can

  • use the rolling condition \(v_\text{cm} = R\omega\);
  • write the kinetic energy of a rolling object as translation plus rotation;
  • find the share of kinetic energy in rotation for each shape;
  • use energy conservation for objects rolling down or up an incline and predict who wins a race.

3.1 Rolling without slipping

A rolling wheel does two things at once: its center moves forward at \(v_\text{cm}\), and it spins at \(\omega\). Rolling without slipping means the point touching the ground is momentarily at rest: it does not skid. In one turn the wheel moves forward exactly one circumference, \(2\pi R\), so

\[ v_\text{cm} = R\omega, \qquad a_\text{cm} = R\alpha, \qquad x_\text{cm} = R\theta . \]

These are constraints that hold only while the object rolls without slipping. A skidding bowling ball, for instance, does not obey them (see the Slipping before rolling tab).

3.2 Kinetic energy of a rolling object

The kinetic energy of any rigid body is the kinetic energy of its center of mass plus the kinetic energy of its rotation about the center of mass (Brown, section 5.7):

\[ K = \underbrace{\tfrac12 Mv_\text{cm}^2}_{\text{translation}} + \underbrace{\tfrac12 I_\text{cm}\omega^2}_{\text{rotation}} . \]

For rolling without slipping, \(\omega = v_\text{cm}/R\) and \(I_\text{cm} = cMR^2\):

\[ K = \tfrac12 Mv^2 + \tfrac12 cMR^2\frac{v^2}{R^2} = \boxed{\ \tfrac12 Mv^2\,(1 + c)\ } \]

So a rolling object carries \((1+c)\) times the kinetic energy of the same object sliding at the same speed. The shares are fixed by the shape alone:

\[ \frac{K_\text{trans}}{K} = \frac{1}{1+c}, \qquad \frac{K_\text{rot}}{K} = \frac{c}{1+c} . \]

At each instant the contact point P is at rest, so the object is turning about P. By the parallel-axis theorem, \(I_P = I_\text{cm} + MR^2 = (1+c)MR^2\), and

\[ K = \tfrac12 I_P\omega^2 = \tfrac12(1+c)MR^2\omega^2 = \tfrac12 Mv^2(1+c), \]

the same result. This also explains Figure 3.1: a point at distance \(d\) from P moves at \(d\omega\), so the top (\(d = 2R\)) moves at \(2v\).

3.3 Rolling on a level surface

On a level floor, with no drag, a rolling object keeps rolling at constant speed. No horizontal force is needed: the floor’s static friction is zero. Even when static friction does act (speeding up, slowing down, on a slope), it does no work on an object that rolls without slipping, because it acts at the contact point, which is not moving (Brown, section 5.7.2).

Example: a bowling ball

A 7.0 kg bowling ball (solid sphere) rolls at 8.0 m/s.

\[ K_\text{trans} = \tfrac12(7.0)(8.0)^2 = 224\ \text{J}, \quad K_\text{rot} = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac25(7.0)(8.0)^2 = 89.6\ \text{J}, \quad K = 314\ \text{J}. \]

The ratio is \(89.6/314 = 28.6\%\), as the table predicts. Its radius was not needed.

Side tab

A bowling ball is not thrown rolling: it is released sliding, and friction makes it roll. What happens in between, and where does energy go? See Slipping before rolling.

3.4 Rolling downhill

An object rolls from rest down an incline, its center dropping a height \(h\). Gravity does work \(Mgh\); the normal force does none (it is perpendicular to the motion); static friction does none (it acts at the stationary contact point). So mechanical energy is conserved:

\[ Mgh = \tfrac12 Mv^2(1+c) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1+c}} . \]

A frictionless sliding block would reach \(\sqrt{2gh}\). A rolling object is slower at the bottom because part of the energy goes into spinning. Its acceleration along the slope is also smaller:

\[ a = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1+c} \qquad\text{(e.g. } \tfrac23 g\sin\theta \text{ for a disk, Brown Example 5.6.1).} \]
Who wins the race?

Released together from the same height, the object with the smallest \(c\) wins: solid sphere, then solid cylinder, then hollow sphere, then hoop. Mass and radius cancel out, so a big heavy solid cylinder and a small light one tie.

How much friction is needed to roll without slipping?

Newton’s second law along the slope, \(Mg\sin\theta - f_s = Ma\), with \(a = g\sin\theta/(1+c)\), gives \(f_s = Mg\sin\theta\,\dfrac{c}{1+c}\). Rolling without slipping requires \(f_s \le \mu_s N = \mu_s Mg\cos\theta\):

\[ \mu_s \ \ge\ \frac{c}{1+c}\tan\theta \]

For a disk this is \(\tfrac13\tan\theta\) (Brown, Example 5.6.1). On a steep or icy slope the object slips, and energy is lost to kinetic friction.

3.5 Rolling uphill

Now an object rolls up an incline with initial speed \(v_0\) and slows down. Energy conservation gives the highest point reached:

\[ \tfrac12 Mv_0^2(1+c) = Mgh_\text{max} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h_\text{max} = \frac{v_0^2\,(1+c)}{2g} . \]
  • Same initial speed: the hoop goes highest. At the same speed it carries the most kinetic energy, because half of it is in rotation, and on the way up the rotational energy is converted into height too.
  • Same initial kinetic energy (same mass): they all reach the same height, \(h = K_0/(Mg)\). Shape decides only how the energy is shared, not how much there is.
Common trap

“Friction always points against the motion.” For an object rolling uphill and slowing down, static friction points up the slope, along the motion: it must slow the spin, and only a force pointing up the slope at the contact point does that. Downhill it also points up the slope. In both cases it does no work.

Side tab

Slipping before rolling

What happens

A bowling ball leaves the hand sliding, with little or no spin: \(v_0 \ne R\omega\). The contact point skids along the lane, so kinetic friction \(f_k = \mu_k Mg\) acts on it, pointing backward. That one force does two things at once:

  • it slows the translation: \(a_\text{cm} = -\mu_k g\);
  • its torque about the center spins the ball up: \(\alpha = \dfrac{f_k R}{I} = \dfrac{\mu_k g}{cR}\).

So \(v\) goes down while \(R\omega\) goes up. The moment they are equal, the contact point stops skidding: the ball rolls without slipping, kinetic friction switches off, and the ball keeps rolling at constant speed.

When does it start rolling?

Starting with \(v = v_0\) and \(\omega = 0\): \(v(t) = v_0 - \mu_k g\,t\) and \(R\omega(t) = \dfrac{\mu_k g}{c}\,t\). Setting them equal:

\[ t_\text{roll} = \frac{c}{1+c}\,\frac{v_0}{\mu_k g}, \qquad v_\text{roll} = \frac{v_0}{1+c} . \]

For a solid sphere (\(c = \tfrac25\)), the ball ends up rolling at \(\tfrac57 v_0\), whatever the friction coefficient: \(\mu_k\) only sets how long it takes.

Where the energy goes

Here friction does work, because the contact point slides. Kinetic energy is lost to heat (and the lane wears):

\[ \frac{K_\text{rolling}}{K_0} = \frac{\tfrac12 M v_\text{roll}^2 (1+c)}{\tfrac12 Mv_0^2} = \frac{1}{1+c} . \]

A solid sphere keeps \(\tfrac57 \approx 71\%\) of its kinetic energy and loses about 29%. Compare with rolling without slipping, where static friction does no work and no energy is lost.

Example

A bowling ball released at \(v_0 = 8.0\ \text{m/s}\) with no spin, \(\mu_k = 0.20\): it starts rolling after \(t = \tfrac{0.4}{1.4}\cdot\tfrac{8.0}{(0.20)(9.8)} = 1.17\ \text{s}\), at \(v = 5.7\ \text{m/s}\), having slid about 8.0 m.

The other way round

A ball with backspin (\(\omega\) opposite to rolling) slows down even faster and can even come back toward you: kinetic friction now has to reverse the spin before rolling can begin. Billiard players use this for the “draw” shot.

Wrap-up

Summary: the main points

1. Rotational kinetic energy

  • A spinning object has kinetic energy even when its center of mass is at rest: every piece moves, at \(v = r\omega\).
  • Adding \(\tfrac12 m_i v_i^2\) over all the pieces gives \(K_\text{rot} = \tfrac12 I\omega^2\), with \(\omega\) in rad/s. It is the same pattern as \(\tfrac12 mv^2\), with \(m \to I\) and \(v \to \omega\).
  • For round objects \(I = cMR^2\): \(c = 1\) (hoop), \(\tfrac23\) (hollow sphere), \(\tfrac12\) (solid cylinder), \(\tfrac25\) (solid sphere). The more mass near the rim, the larger \(c\).
  • Rankings depend on what is held equal: with the same \(M\), \(R\) and \(\omega\), \(K_\text{rot} \propto c\); with the same \(M\) and \(\omega\), \(K_\text{rot} \propto cR^2\); with the same energy, the smallest \(I\) spins fastest.

2. The work–energy theorem

  • For a particle: \(W_\text{net} = \Delta K = \tfrac12 mv_f^2 - \tfrac12 mv_i^2\).
  • A torque does work as the object turns: \(W = \tau\,\Delta\theta\) for a constant torque (angle in radians). A force along the radius does no rotational work.
  • For rotation: \(W_\text{net} = \Delta K_\text{rot} = \tfrac12 I\omega_f^2 - \tfrac12 I\omega_i^2\). Torque and time give \(\omega(t)\); torque and angle give the energy.
  • The same work on two objects gives the same kinetic energy, but not the same \(\omega\).
  • Negative work (a friction or braking torque) removes kinetic energy, which becomes heat. Rotational power is \(P = \tau\omega\).

3. Rolling motion

  • Rolling without slipping: \(v_\text{cm} = R\omega\); the contact point is momentarily at rest and the top moves at \(2v\).
  • Kinetic energy = translation + rotation: \(K = \tfrac12 Mv^2 + \tfrac12 I_\text{cm}\omega^2 = \tfrac12 Mv^2(1 + c)\).
  • The shape fixes the split: \(\tfrac{1}{1+c}\) in translation and \(\tfrac{c}{1+c}\) in rotation (hoop 50/50, hollow sphere 60/40, solid cylinder 67/33, solid sphere 71/29).
  • Static friction on a rolling object does no work, so mechanical energy is conserved.
  • Downhill from rest: \(v = \sqrt{2gh/(1+c)}\) and \(a = g\sin\theta/(1+c)\). The smallest \(c\) wins: solid sphere, solid cylinder, hollow sphere, hoop. Mass and radius do not matter.
  • Uphill: \(h_\text{max} = v_0^2(1+c)/2g\). With the same initial speed the hoop climbs highest; with the same initial kinetic energy they all reach the same height.
  • On a slope, static friction points up the slope whether the object rolls up or down.

Slipping before rolling

  • An object launched sliding without spin skids: kinetic friction slows \(v\) and spins up \(R\omega\) until \(v = R\omega\), then it rolls.
  • It ends up rolling at \(v_0/(1+c)\) (\(\tfrac57 v_0\) for a solid sphere) and loses the fraction \(\tfrac{c}{1+c}\) of its kinetic energy to heat: kinetic friction does work because the contact point slides.

Common traps

  • Using \(\omega\) in rpm or rev/s instead of rad/s, or an angle in revolutions in \(W = \tau\,\Delta\theta\).
  • Forgetting the rotational term for a rolling object, which gives the frictionless-block answer \(\sqrt{2gh}\).
  • Using the wrong \(I\) for the shape (a basketball is a hollow sphere), or about the wrong axis.
  • Thinking that heavier or bigger objects roll down faster: only the shape factor \(c\) matters.
  • Thinking static friction must point against the motion, or that it does work on an object rolling without slipping.

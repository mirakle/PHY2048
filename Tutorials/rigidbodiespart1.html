Rigid bodies: Kinematics and Dynamics

So far every object has been a point. Real objects have size and shape, and where a force acts matters as much as how big it is. These notes build three tools: the center of mass, torque, and the moment of inertia.

What is a rigid body?

A rigid body is an object whose shape never changes: the distance between any two of its points stays fixed. No real object is perfectly rigid, but a wrench, a door or a meter stick comes very close for the forces we will use.

A rigid body can do two things at once. Its center of mass moves as if the whole mass were a single particle pushed by the net external force, and the body can rotate about its center of mass. Throw a wrench spinning across the floor: one special point slides in a straight line while the rest of the wrench whirls around it.

Key idea

Rigid-body motion = motion of the center of mass (Newton’s second law, \(\vec F_\text{net} = M\vec a_\text{cm}\)) + rotation about it (governed by torque, Part 2, and the moment of inertia, Part 3).

Ground rules

  • SI units throughout; \(g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\).
  • Axes form a right-handed set: \(x\) to the right, \(y\) into the page or up, \(z\) completing the set. The unit vectors along \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) are \(\hat\imath\), \(\hat\jmath\) and \(\hat k\), with \(\hat\imath \times \hat\jmath = \hat k\); a vector is written \(\vec A = A_x\hat\imath + A_y\hat\jmath + A_z\hat k\).
  • In plane problems, a torque that tends to turn things counterclockwise is positive.
  • Colour code, always paired with a label or a line style: position \(\vec r\) (blue), force \(\vec F\) (crimson), torque \(\vec\tau\) (green, thicker arrow), center of mass (orange target), axis of rotation (purple).

Part 1

Center of mass

After this part you can

  • compute the center of mass of a set of point masses in one and two dimensions;
  • locate the center of mass of simple uniform shapes using symmetry and a table of standard results;
  • find the center of mass of composite shapes by splitting them into pieces;
  • explain when the center of gravity and the center of mass are the same point, and when they are not.

1.1 Center of mass of point masses

Take a light rod with a 2 kg mass at one end and a 6 kg mass at the other. Where do you put your finger to balance it? Not in the middle: closer to the heavy end. The balance point is a mass-weighted average of the positions.

For particles on a line,

\[ x_\text{cm} = \frac{m_1x_1 + m_2x_2 + \dots}{m_1+m_2+\dots} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_i m_i x_i, \qquad M = \sum_i m_i . \]

In two or three dimensions, apply the same rule to each coordinate, or write it once as a vector:

\[ \vec r_\text{cm} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_i m_i\,\vec r_i \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_\text{cm} = \frac{\sum m_i x_i}{M},\ \ y_\text{cm} = \frac{\sum m_i y_i}{M},\ \ z_\text{cm} = \frac{\sum m_i z_i}{M}. \]
Key idea

The center of mass sits closer to the heavier masses. It does not have to sit on any mass, or even on the object.

Example: three masses in a plane

Masses \(m_A = 2\ \text{kg}\) at \((1, 1)\) m, \(m_B = 4\ \text{kg}\) at \((8, 2)\) m and \(m_C = 1\ \text{kg}\) at \((4, 5)\) m. With \(M = 7\ \text{kg}\),

\[ x_\text{cm} = \frac{(2)(1)+(4)(8)+(1)(4)}{7} = \frac{38}{7} = 5.43\ \text{m},\qquad y_\text{cm} = \frac{(2)(1)+(4)(2)+(1)(5)}{7} = \frac{15}{7} = 2.14\ \text{m}. \]

1.2 Center of mass of an extended object

A solid object is a huge number of tiny pieces, far too many to add up one by one. For the shapes in this course you will find the center of mass in one of two ways: symmetry, or the table of standard results below. (Behind the table is an integral; see the box at the end of this section.)

Use symmetry first

If a uniform object has a line or plane of symmetry, its center of mass lies on it. Two symmetry lines? The CM is where they cross. That gives the center of a uniform rod, disk, rectangle, sphere or ring straight away.

Example: a uniform rod

A uniform rod of length \(L\) is symmetric about its middle, so \(x_\text{cm} = L/2\), measured from either end.

Example: shapes without enough symmetry

A right-triangle plate has no line of symmetry, so you read its CM from the table: \(\big(\tfrac b3, \tfrac h3\big)\), a third of the way in from the right-angle corner along each leg. A half-disk has one symmetry line, so its CM is on that line; the table tells you how far up: \(4R/(3\pi)\).

Where the table results come from For information, not tested

Cut the object into pieces of mass \(dm\) at positions \(\vec r\); the sum becomes an integral,

\[ \vec r_\text{cm} = \frac{1}{M}\int \vec r\,dm, \]

with \(dm = \lambda\,dx\) for a rod (\(\lambda\) in kg/m), \(\sigma\,dA\) for a plate (kg/m²) or \(\rho\,dV\) for a solid (kg/m³).

Uniform rod (\(dm = (M/L)\,dx\)): \(x_\text{cm} = \frac1M\int_0^L x\,\frac{M}{L}\,dx = \frac L2\), as symmetry promised.

A rod that gets heavier along its length (\(\lambda = \alpha x\)): \(M = \tfrac12\alpha L^2\) and \(x_\text{cm} = \frac1M\int_0^L x(\alpha x)\,dx = \frac{2L}{3}\), toward the heavy end.

Right-triangle plate with corners \((0,0)\), \((b,0)\), \((0,h)\): vertical strips have area \(dA = h(1 - x/b)\,dx\), so

\[ x_\text{cm} = \frac{2}{bh}\int_0^b x\,h\Big(1-\frac xb\Big)dx = \frac b3 , \]

and horizontal strips give \(y_\text{cm} = h/3\).

Centers of mass of uniform simple shapes
ShapeLocation of the CM
rod, rectangle, disk, ring, sphere, cylindergeometric center (symmetry)
triangular platewhere the medians cross; \(\big(\tfrac b3,\tfrac h3\big)\) for a right triangle
semicircular wire (arc), radius \(R\)\(2R/\pi \approx 0.64R\) from the center, on the symmetry line

solid hemisphere\(3R/8\) above the flat face
solid cone, height \(h\)\(h/4\) above the base, on the axis

Notice the semicircular wire: its CM is in empty space, inside the curve. A boomerang, a ring and a horseshoe all have the same property.

1.3 Composite shapes: L, T, U and F

Most objects are built from simple pieces. You don’t need a new integral: treat each piece as a single particle sitting at its own center of mass, with the piece’s mass.

\[ x_\text{cm} = \frac{\sum_i m_i\,x_{\text{cm},i}}{\sum_i m_i}, \qquad\text{and for a uniform sheet}\quad x_\text{cm} = \frac{\sum_i A_i\,x_{\text{cm},i}}{\sum_i A_i}. \]

The routine:

  1. Split the shape into rectangles (or other shapes whose CM you know). Pieces must not overlap.
  2. For each piece, write its area \(A_i\) and the coordinates of its center.
  3. Take the area-weighted average of the \(x\) values, then of the \(y\) values.
  4. Check: symmetry, and is the answer inside the bounding box and closer to the bigger pieces?

Origin at the bottom-left corner of each letter; all pieces are cut from the same uniform sheet.

L: 8 wide, 10 tall

Upright \(2\times10\): \(A=20\), center \((1,5)\). Foot \(6\times2\): \(A=12\), center \((5,1)\).

\(x_\text{cm} = \frac{20(1)+12(5)}{32} = 2.50\), \(y_\text{cm} = \frac{20(5)+12(1)}{32} = 3.50\).

Off the material: in the inside corner.

T: 10 wide, 12 tall

Top bar \(10\times2\): \(A=20\), center \((5,11)\). Stem \(2\times10\): \(A=20\), center \((5,5)\).

Symmetry gives \(x_\text{cm} = 5\); \(y_\text{cm} = \frac{20(11)+20(5)}{40} = 8.00\).

On the stem, 2 cm below the top bar.

U: 10 wide, 10 tall

Two arms \(2\times10\): \(A=20\) each, centers \((1,5)\), \((9,5)\). Base \(6\times2\): \(A=12\), center \((5,1)\).

\(x_\text{cm} = 5\) (symmetry); \(y_\text{cm} = \frac{20(5)+12(1)+20(5)}{52} = 4.08\).

Off the material: in the gap between the arms.

F: 8 wide, 12 tall

Spine \(2\times12\): \(A=24\), \((1,6)\). Top arm \(6\times2\): \(A=12\), \((5,11)\). Middle arm \(4\times2\) (from \(y=5\) to 7): \(A=8\), \((4,6)\).

\(x_\text{cm} = \frac{24+60+32}{44} = 2.64\), \(y_\text{cm} = \frac{144+132+48}{44} = 7.36\).

Off the material: just above the middle arm.

Shapes made of thin rods or wires: weight by length

If the letter is bent from uniform wire, each piece’s mass is proportional to its length. Example: a T made of wire with total mass \(M = 0.50\ \text{kg}\), a 20 cm top bar and a 30 cm stem hanging from its middle. Measure from the left end of the top bar, with \(y\) up.

  • Top bar: \(m_1 = \tfrac{20}{50}(0.50) = 0.20\ \text{kg}\), center at \((10, 0)\) cm.
  • Stem: \(m_2 = \tfrac{30}{50}(0.50) = 0.30\ \text{kg}\), center at \((10, -15)\) cm (below the bar, so negative).
\[ x_\text{cm} = \frac{(0.20)(10) + (0.30)(10)}{0.50} = 10\ \text{cm},\qquad y_\text{cm} = \frac{(0.20)(0) + (0.30)(-15)}{0.50} = -9.0\ \text{cm}. \]

The CM is on the stem, 9 cm below the bar: closer to the bar than the stem’s own center, because the bar adds mass at the top.

Another route: subtract a hole (negative area)

The U is a full \(10\times10\) square with a \(6\times8\) hole cut out (the hole runs from \(y=2\) to \(y=10\), center \((5,6)\)). Count the hole as a piece of negative area:

\[ y_\text{cm} = \frac{(100)(5) + (-48)(6)}{100 - 48} = \frac{500-288}{52} = 4.08\ \text{cm}, \]

the same answer. This trick is handy for plates with holes drilled in them.

1.4 Center of mass versus center of gravity

The center of mass depends only on how mass is spread out. The center of gravity (CG) is the point where the whole weight of the object effectively acts: if you replace all the little weights \(dm\,\vec g\) by one force \(M\vec g\) placed at the CG, you get the same torque about any point.

For an object small enough that \(\vec g\) is the same everywhere on it, the two points coincide. The torque of gravity about the origin is

\[ \vec\tau_\text{grav} = \sum_i \vec r_i\times(m_i\vec g) = \Big(\sum_i m_i\vec r_i\Big)\times\vec g = M\vec r_\text{cm}\times\vec g = \vec r_\text{cm}\times(M\vec g). \]

So with uniform \(\vec g\), the weight acts at the center of mass. That is why we draw the weight of a rod at its middle (Part 2).

When do they differ?

Only when \(\vec g\) changes over the object, in size or direction. Then parts where gravity is stronger count for more, and the CG shifts toward them. For anything in a lab the shift is far too small to measure; for very tall structures and for moons it is real. The Moon’s CG is slightly closer to Earth than its CM, which is part of why it keeps the same face toward us.

Example: how big is the shift for a skyscraper?

Model an 828 m tower as a uniform vertical rod. Near Earth’s surface \(g(y)\approx g_0(1 - 2y/R_E)\), with \(R_E = 6.37\times10^6\ \text{m}\). The center of gravity is the weight-weighted average height: parts where \(g\) is larger count for more.

Show the derivation For information, not tested

The weight-weighted average height is \(y_\text{cg} = \int y\,g\,dm \big/ \int g\,dm\).

With \(dm = \lambda\,dy\),

\[ y_\text{cg} = \frac{\int_0^L y\,(1-2y/R_E)\,dy}{\int_0^L (1-2y/R_E)\,dy} = \frac{\tfrac{L^2}{2}-\tfrac{2L^3}{3R_E}}{L-\tfrac{L^2}{R_E}} \approx \frac L2\Big(1-\frac{L}{3R_E}\Big) = \frac{L}{2} - \frac{L^2}{6R_E}, \]

keeping only first-order terms in the tiny ratio \(L/R_E\).

The shift is \(\dfrac{L^2}{6R_E} = \dfrac{(828)^2}{6(6.37\times10^6)} \approx 0.018\ \text{m}\): under 2 cm, for a tower almost a kilometre tall. For everything else in this course, the CG and the CM are the same point.

Finding the center of gravity by hanging

An object hung from a single point comes to rest with its CG directly below the pivot: any other position leaves a torque from gravity that swings it back. Hang a flat cut-out from one point and draw the vertical line through the pivot (a plumb line); hang it from a second point and draw another. The CG is where the lines cross, even if that point is off the material, as for the L.

Common trap

“If a bat balances at a point, the two sides have equal mass.” No. Balance means equal torques. The thick end has more mass, but its mass sits closer to the balance point; the thin handle has less mass, farther away. Cut the bat at the balance point and the barrel piece is heavier.

Part 2

Torque from a constant force

After this part you can

  • compute the size of a torque three equivalent ways: \(rF\sin\theta\), lever arm × force, distance × perpendicular force;
  • compute \(\vec\tau = \vec r\times\vec F\) from components and find its direction with the right-hand rule;
  • find the torque of gravity on a rod by placing its weight at the center of mass;
  • add torques with signs to find a net torque.

2.1 Where a force acts matters

Push on a door near the handle and it swings open easily. Push with the same force near the hinges and it barely moves. Push straight toward the hinge line and nothing happens at all. Same force, three different turning effects. That turning effect is the torque.

Torque depends on three things: how big the force is, how far from the pivot it acts, and in which direction it points.

2.2 The size of a torque

Let \(\vec r\) run from the pivot to the point where the force acts, and let \(\theta\) be the angle between \(\vec r\) and \(\vec F\). To measure it, extend the line of \(\vec r\) beyond the point where the force acts and turn counterclockwise from that extension to \(\vec F\) (Figure 2.2). The size of the torque is

\[ \tau = rF\sin\theta . \]

Two ways to read the \(\sin\theta\), both shown in Figure 2.1:

  • Perpendicular force: only the component of \(\vec F\) perpendicular to \(\vec r\), \(F_\perp = F\sin\theta\), turns the object. The parallel part just pulls on the pivot. So \(\tau = rF_\perp\).
  • Lever arm: extend the force into its line of action. The perpendicular distance from the pivot to that line is the lever arm (or moment arm), \(r_\perp = r\sin\theta\). So \(\tau = r_\perp F\).
Key idea

A force whose line of action passes through the pivot produces no torque, however large it is. Torque is largest when the force is perpendicular to \(\vec r\).

Units trap

Torque is measured in newton-metres, N·m. That is dimensionally the same as a joule, but torque is not energy: never write a torque in joules.

2.3 Torque as a vector

A torque has a direction: it tends to turn the object one way or the other about some axis. The vector definition captures both:

\[ \vec\tau = \vec r\times\vec F, \qquad |\vec\tau| = rF\sin\theta . \]

The torque is perpendicular to the plane containing \(\vec r\) and \(\vec F\). Two equivalent versions of the right-hand rule tell you which of the two perpendicular directions it takes. Always use your right hand.

  • Three-finger rule (Figure 2.3). Point your index finger along \(\vec r\) and your middle finger along \(\vec F\). Your thumb, held perpendicular to both, points along \(\vec\tau = \vec r\times\vec F\).
  • Curled-hand rule (Figure 2.4). Curl the fingers of your right hand from \(\vec r\) toward \(\vec F\), through the smaller angle between them. This is the sense in which the force tends to turn the object. Your extended thumb points along \(\vec\tau\), along the axis the object tends to turn about.
Check yourself

Both rules must give the same answer. For a plane problem viewed from above: if the force tends to turn the object counterclockwise, your thumb points up, out of the page (\(+\hat k\)); clockwise, it points down, into the page (\(-\hat k\)). Using your left hand by mistake flips the answer.

In components,

\[ \vec\tau = (r_yF_z - r_zF_y)\,\hat\imath + (r_zF_x - r_xF_z)\,\hat\jmath + (r_xF_y - r_yF_x)\,\hat k . \]

For a plane problem (\(\vec r\) and \(\vec F\) both in the \(xy\) plane), only \(\tau_z = r_xF_y - r_yF_x\) survives: positive is counterclockwise seen from \(+z\), negative is clockwise.

Example: torque from components

A force \(\vec F = (0,\,3,\,4)\ \text{N}\) acts at \(\vec r = (0.5,\,0.2,\,0)\ \text{m}\) from a pivot.

\[ \vec\tau = \big((0.2)(4) - 0\big)\hat\imath + \big(0 - (0.5)(4)\big)\hat\jmath + \big((0.5)(3) - 0\big)\hat k = (0.8,\,-2.0,\,1.5)\ \text{N·m}, \]

with magnitude \(\sqrt{0.8^2+2.0^2+1.5^2} = 2.6\ \text{N·m}\). Check: \(\vec\tau\cdot\vec r = 0.4 - 0.4 + 0 = 0\), so \(\vec\tau\) is perpendicular to \(\vec r\), as it must be.

2.4 3-D torque explorer

A bar lies along the \(x\) axis, pivoted at the origin on a ball joint so it can turn any way. A constant force acts on it at distance \(r\) from the pivot. Set the force’s size, its angle \(\theta\) to the bar, and its tilt \(\varphi\) out of the \(xy\) plane, and watch the torque vector.

Predict. Keep the force perpendicular to the bar (\(\theta = 90^\circ\)) and tilt it from \(\varphi = 0^\circ\) to \(90^\circ\). Does the size of the torque change? Does its direction?

2.5 Examples with rods

Example 1: a wrench

You pull with 80 N at the end of a 0.25 m wrench. Perpendicular to the handle: \(\tau = (0.25)(80)\sin 90^\circ = 20\ \text{N·m}\). At \(60^\circ\) to the handle: \(\tau = (0.25)(80)\sin 60^\circ = 17.3\ \text{N·m}\). Along the handle: zero.

Example 2: a horizontal rod and its own weight

A uniform rod (mass \(M = 2.0\ \text{kg}\), length \(L = 1.2\ \text{m}\)) is held horizontal by a hinge at one end. Every bit of the rod is pulled down, but (Section 1.4) the total effect of gravity is the weight \(Mg\) acting at the center of mass, a distance \(L/2\) from the hinge.

\[ \tau = \frac L2\,Mg = (0.60)(2.0)(9.8) = 11.8\ \text{N·m}\quad\text{(clockwise, so } \tau_z = -11.8\ \text{N·m)}. \]
Show that “weight at the CM” gives the same answer as adding up every piece For information, not tested

A piece \(dx\) at distance \(x\) from the hinge has weight \(dW = (M/L)g\,dx\) and lever arm \(x\):

\[ \tau = \int_0^L x\,\frac{M}{L}g\,dx = \frac{Mg}{L}\cdot\frac{L^2}{2} = Mg\,\frac L2 . \]

Example 3: the same rod, tilted

Raise the rod to \(30^\circ\) above horizontal. The weight still points straight down from the CM, but now it makes a \(60^\circ\) angle with the rod, so

\[ \tau = \frac L2\,Mg\sin 60^\circ = \frac L2\,Mg\cos 30^\circ = (11.8)(0.866) = 10.2\ \text{N·m}. \]

Lever-arm view: the horizontal distance from the hinge to the vertical line through the CM is \((L/2)\cos30^\circ = 0.52\ \text{m}\). Hold the rod straight up and the lever arm, and the torque, drop to zero.

2.6 Net torque

When several forces act, add their torques about the same pivot, with signs (counterclockwise positive in a plane problem):

\[ \tau_\text{net} = \sum_i \tau_i . \]

The 30 N force on the left tends to turn the rod counterclockwise: \(\tau_1 = +(1.0)(30) = +30\ \text{N·m}\). The 20 N force on the right tends to turn it clockwise: \(\tau_2 = -(0.6)(20) = -12\ \text{N·m}\). So

\[ \tau_\text{net} = +30 - 12 = +18\ \text{N·m}\quad\text{(counterclockwise)}. \]

To balance it you need another \(-18\ \text{N·m}\): for example 25 N pushing down at \(18/25 = 0.72\ \text{m}\) right of the pivot. Balanced torques (and balanced forces) are the subject of static equilibrium, coming later.

Key idea

Torque depends on the pivot you choose. Always say “torque about point O”, and use the same point for every force in a sum.

Newton’s second law for rotation

After this part you can

  • describe a rotation with an angle, an angular velocity and an angular acceleration, and use the constant-\(\alpha\) equations;
  • tell angular (and tangential) acceleration apart from centripetal acceleration;
  • build \(\tau_\text{net} = I\alpha\) by analogy with \(\vec F_\text{net} = m\vec a\);
  • compute moments of inertia of point masses, read those of simple shapes from the table, and use the parallel-axis theorem;
  • solve a full problem: torque, then \(I\), then \(\alpha\), then angle and number of turns.

3.1 Describing a rotation: angle, angular velocity, angular acceleration

For a point particle, Newton’s second law links force to the rate of change of velocity. To write the same law for rotation we first need rotational versions of position, velocity and acceleration.

Take a rigid body turning about a fixed axis (the \(z\) axis). Mark any line painted on the body; the angle \(\theta\) it makes with the \(x\) axis tells you how far the body has turned. Angles are measured in radians, so that a point at distance \(r\) from the axis travels an arc length \(s = r\theta\). One revolution is \(2\pi\) rad. As in Part 2, counterclockwise (seen from \(+z\)) is positive.

\[ \omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt}\ \ (\text{rad/s}), \qquad \alpha = \frac{d\omega}{dt} = \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}\ \ (\text{rad/s}^2). \]

\(\omega\) is the angular velocity and \(\alpha\) the angular acceleration. Because the body is rigid, every point of it has the same \(\theta\), \(\omega\) and \(\alpha\), even though points far from the axis move faster: \(v = r\omega\).

When \(\alpha\) is constant, the equations are the ones you know from 1-D kinematics, letter for letter:

Constant acceleration: linear and angular
Linear (constant \(a\))Angular (constant \(\alpha\))
\(v = v_0 + at\)\(\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t\)
\(x = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac12at^2\)\(\theta = \theta_0 + \omega_0t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2\)
\(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)\)\(\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha(\theta - \theta_0)\)

The number of revolutions in an angle \(\Delta\theta\) is \(n = |\Delta\theta|/2\pi\). The sign of \(\Delta\theta\) only tells you the sense of rotation.

Angular acceleration is not centripetal acceleration

You met an acceleration for circular motion before: the centripetal acceleration \(a_c = v^2/r = r\omega^2\), pointing to the center. (Need a reminder? Open the Refresher: circular motion tab.) It is a different thing from \(\alpha\):

Three different “accelerations” for a point at distance \(r\) from the axis
QuantityFormulaDirectionWhat it does
angular acceleration\(\alpha = d\omega/dt\) (rad/s²)along the axis (\(\pm\hat k\))describes how fast the whole body spins up or slows down
tangential acceleration\(a_t = r\alpha\) (m/s²)along the path (tangent)changes the point’s speed
centripetal acceleration\(a_c = r\omega^2 = v^2/r\) (m/s²)toward the axischanges the direction of the point’s velocity

The total acceleration of the point is the vector sum of the tangential and centripetal parts, with size \(a = \sqrt{a_t^2 + a_c^2}\).

Common trap

“The wheel turns at a steady rate, so nothing is accelerating.” In uniform rotation \(\alpha = 0\) and \(a_t = 0\), but every point still has a centripetal acceleration toward the axis. Conversely, a wheel just starting from rest has \(\omega = 0\), so \(a_c = 0\), but \(\alpha\) and \(a_t\) are not zero.

3.2 Newton’s second law for rotation

For a point particle, \(\vec F_\text{net} = m\vec a\): a net force produces an acceleration, and the mass measures how hard the particle is to accelerate. We now build the rotational version in three steps.

Step 1: one particle on a light rod

A particle of mass \(m\) is fixed at the end of a massless rod of length \(r\), pivoted at the other end. A force \(\vec F\) acts on the particle at angle \(\varphi\) to the rod. Split \(\vec F\) into a radial part \(F\cos\varphi\) (along the rod; the rod pushes back and the particle cannot move that way) and a tangential part \(F_t = F\sin\varphi\). Only the tangential part changes the speed:

\[ F_t = m\,a_t = m\,r\alpha . \]

Multiply both sides by \(r\). The left side becomes the torque of Part 2:

\[ \tau = rF\sin\varphi = (mr^2)\,\alpha . \]

Step 2: many particles moving together

A rigid body is many particles at distances \(r_1, r_2, \dots\) from the axis, all sharing the same \(\alpha\). Write step 1 for each particle and add. The internal forces between particles come in equal and opposite pairs along the line joining them, so their torques cancel; only external torques are left:

\[ \tau_\text{net} = \big(m_1r_1^2 + m_2r_2^2 + \dots\big)\,\alpha = \Big(\sum_i m_ir_i^2\Big)\alpha . \]

Step 3: name the sum

The sum is the moment of inertia \(I\), and the result is Newton’s second law for rotation about a fixed axis:

\[ \boxed{\ \tau_\text{net} = I\alpha\ } \qquad I = \sum_i m_ir_i^2 . \]

Key idea

Mass is a property of the object alone. Moment of inertia is a property of the object and the axis: what counts is how far each bit of mass is from the axis, and it counts with the square of that distance. Always compute \(\tau\) and \(I\) about the same axis.

3.3 The moment of inertia

Every moment of inertia is built from three ingredients: the mass, a size squared (a length or a radius, sometimes a combination such as \(a^2 + b^2\)), and a number set by the shape and the axis. For example, \(I = \tfrac12MR^2\) for a disk: mass \(M\), size \(R^2\), number \(\tfrac12\).

Point masses: add up \(mr^2\)

Two 0.50 kg balls sit at the ends of a light 1.0 m rod.

  • Axis through the middle: each ball is 0.50 m away, so \(I = 2(0.50)(0.50)^2 = 0.25\ \text{kg·m}^2\).
  • Axis through one ball: that ball contributes nothing, the other is 1.0 m away, so \(I = (0.50)(1.0)^2 = 0.50\ \text{kg·m}^2\).

Same object, same mass, twice the moment of inertia: the axis matters.

Extended objects: use the table

For a solid object the sum \(\sum mr^2\) has far too many terms to add by hand. The results for common shapes are collected in the table below; you will use them directly, together with the parallel-axis theorem (Section 3.4).

Two of them you can understand without any calculus:

  • Thin ring (hoop) about its axis: every bit of mass is at the same distance \(R\), so \(I = \sum m_iR^2 = MR^2\).
  • Solid disk about its axis: the same mass and radius, but much of the mass sits close to the axis, so \(I\) is smaller: \(\tfrac12MR^2\), half the hoop’s value.

Likewise a rod about its end (\(\tfrac13ML^2\)) has four times the moment of inertia of the same rod about its center (\(\tfrac1{12}ML^2\)): moving the axis to the end puts much more mass far from it.

Where the table results come from For information, not tested

Cut the object into pieces \(dm\) at perpendicular distance \(r_\perp\) from the axis; the sum becomes \(I = \int r_\perp^2\,dm\).

Rod about its center (\(x\) from \(-L/2\) to \(L/2\), \(dm = (M/L)\,dx\)): \(I_\text{cm} = \int_{-L/2}^{L/2} x^2\,\frac ML\,dx = \frac{1}{12}ML^2\).

Rod about one end: \(I_\text{end} = \int_0^L x^2\frac ML\,dx = \frac13ML^2\).

Solid disk: slice it into rings of radius \(r\) and width \(dr\), with mass \(dm = \dfrac{M}{\pi R^2}\,2\pi r\,dr\):

\[ I = \int_0^R r^2\,\frac{2M}{R^2}\,r\,dr = \frac12MR^2 . \]

3.4 The parallel-axis theorem

3.4.1 Statement

If you know \(I_\text{cm}\) about an axis through the center of mass, then about any parallel axis a distance \(d\) away,

\[ I = I_\text{cm} + Md^2 . \]
Show the derivation For information, not tested

Put the CM at the origin and let both axes point along \(z\); the new axis passes through \((d, 0)\) in the \(xy\) plane. A piece at \((x, y)\) is at squared distance \((x-d)^2 + y^2\) from the new axis:

\[ I = \int\big[(x-d)^2+y^2\big]dm = \int(x^2+y^2)\,dm \;-\; 2d\int x\,dm \;+\; d^2\int dm . \]

The first integral is \(I_\text{cm}\). The middle one is \(M x_\text{cm} = 0\), because the origin is the CM: that is exactly why one of the two axes must pass through the CM. The last is \(Md^2\).

  • The axes must be parallel. The theorem does not connect, say, a rod’s “perpendicular” and “along the rod” axes.
  • Since \(Md^2 \ge 0\), the axis through the CM gives the smallest moment of inertia of all parallel axes.
  • \(I\) grows with the square of the offset: double \(d\) and the \(Md^2\) term quadruples.
Common trap

The theorem always starts from the CM axis. You cannot go from the end of a rod to some other point by adding \(Md^2\) with \(d\) measured from the end. Go back to the CM first: \(I_\text{cm} = I_\text{end} - M(L/2)^2\), then add \(Md^2\) for the new axis.

3.4.2 Examples

Rod about its end, again. From the center, \(d = L/2\): \(I_\text{end} = \tfrac1{12}ML^2 + M\big(\tfrac L2\big)^2 = \tfrac13ML^2\). It matches the table value in Section 3.3. For \(M = 2.0\ \text{kg}\), \(L = 1.2\ \text{m}\): \(I_\text{cm} = 0.24\ \text{kg·m}^2\) and \(I_\text{end} = 0.96\ \text{kg·m}^2\).

Rod about a point a quarter of the way along. The axis is \(L/4\) from the center: \(I = \tfrac1{12}ML^2 + M\big(\tfrac L4\big)^2 = \tfrac{7}{48}ML^2\).

Disk about a point on its rim (axis perpendicular to the disk, \(d = R\)): \(I = \tfrac12MR^2 + MR^2 = \tfrac32MR^2\).

Meter stick pivoted at the 20 cm mark (0.150 kg, 1.00 m, so \(d = 0.30\ \text{m}\) from the CM at 50 cm):

\[ I = \tfrac1{12}(0.150)(1.00)^2 + (0.150)(0.30)^2 = 0.0125 + 0.0135 = 0.026\ \text{kg·m}^2 . \]

Is a pendulum bob a point mass? A solid sphere (0.50 kg, radius 0.050 m) hangs with its center 0.80 m below the pivot. Exactly: \(I = \tfrac25(0.50)(0.050)^2 + (0.50)(0.80)^2 = 0.3205\ \text{kg·m}^2\). The point-mass value, 0.3200, is only 0.16% low: the point-mass model is fine when the object is small compared with \(d\).

3.5 Worked example: spinning up a bar

A force of constant magnitude \(F = 4.0\ \text{N}\) is applied perpendicular to a bar of length \(L = 1.2\ \text{m}\) and mass \(M = 2.4\ \text{kg}\), at its free end. The bar is held at the end O by a frictionless pivot and lies on a smooth horizontal table, so gravity produces no torque about the pivot. The force stays perpendicular to the bar and acts for \(t = 3.0\ \text{s}\), starting from rest. How many revolutions does the bar complete? (Treat the bar as a thin rod: only its length matters.)

Strategy. A constant torque gives a constant angular acceleration. So: find \(\tau\), find \(I\), get \(\alpha = \tau/I\), then the angle from \(\theta = \theta_0 + \omega_0t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2\), and divide by \(2\pi\).

Step 1: the torque

\(\tau = rF\sin\varphi\), with \(r = L = 1.2\ \text{m}\) and the force perpendicular to the bar. The force turns the bar clockwise, so the torque is negative:

\[ \tau = -(1.2\ \text{m})(4.0\ \text{N}) = -4.8\ \text{N·m}. \]
Step 2: the moment of inertia

The axis is at the end, not through the CM, so use the parallel-axis theorem with \(d = L/2\):

\[ I = \tfrac1{12}ML^2 + M\big(\tfrac L2\big)^2 = \tfrac13ML^2 = \tfrac13(2.4)(1.2)^2 = 1.152\ \text{kg·m}^2 . \]
Step 3: the angular acceleration \[ \alpha = \frac{\tau}{I} = \frac{-4.8\ \text{N·m}}{1.152\ \text{kg·m}^2} = -4.17\ \text{rad/s}^2 . \]

The negative sign means the bar speeds up clockwise.

Step 4: the angle and the number of revolutions

From rest, with \(\theta_0 = 0\) and \(\omega_0 = 0\):

\[ \theta = \tfrac12\alpha t^2 = \tfrac12(-4.17)(3.0)^2 = -18.8\ \text{rad}, \qquad n = \frac{|\theta|}{2\pi} = \frac{18.8}{2\pi} = 2.98 . \]

The bar completes almost exactly 3 revolutions, clockwise.

Step 5 (extra): how fast is it turning at the end? \[ \omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t = (-4.17)(3.0) = -12.5\ \text{rad/s}, \]

about 2.0 revolutions per second, clockwise. The tip of the bar then moves at \(|v| = L|\omega| = 15\ \text{m/s}\), with a centripetal acceleration \(L\omega^2 \approx 190\ \text{m/s}^2\) and a tangential acceleration of only \(L|\alpha| = 5.0\ \text{m/s}^2\).

Refresher

Uniform circular motion

A short review of circular motion from the kinematics notes, so that you can tell centripetal acceleration apart from the angular acceleration of Part 3.

Uniform circular motion

A particle moves around a circle of radius \(r\) at constant speed \(v\). Its velocity is not constant: it is always tangent to the circle, so its direction keeps turning.

  • Period (time for one turn): \(T = 2\pi r/v\).
  • Angular velocity: \(\omega = 2\pi/T\), constant, in rad/s.
  • Speed: \(v = r\omega\).

Centripetal acceleration

Because the velocity changes direction, the particle accelerates even at constant speed. Its acceleration points toward the center (“centripetal” means center-seeking) and has size

\[ a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = r\omega^2 = v\omega . \]
Show where \(v^2/r\) comes from

In a short time \(\Delta t\) the particle turns through \(\Delta\theta = \omega\Delta t\). Its velocity, always perpendicular to the radius, turns through the same angle, so the velocity changes by \(\Delta v \approx v\,\Delta\theta\), directed toward the center. Then \(a = \lim \Delta v/\Delta t = v\,d\theta/dt = v\omega = v^2/r\).

Example: a ball on a string

A ball on a 0.80 m string makes 2.0 turns per second. Then \(\omega = 2\pi(2.0) = 12.6\ \text{rad/s}\), \(v = r\omega = 10.1\ \text{m/s}\), and \(a_c = r\omega^2 = (0.80)(12.6)^2 = 126\ \text{m/s}^2\), about 13 times \(g\), toward the hand.

There is no “centripetal force”

Newton’s second law along the radius says that the net inward force must equal \(ma_c = mv^2/r\). That net force is made of ordinary forces: tension, gravity, friction, a normal force. \(mv^2/r\) is what they must add up to, not an extra force to draw on a free-body diagram.

When the speed changes

If the particle speeds up or slows down along the circle, it also has a tangential acceleration \(a_t = dv/dt = r\alpha\), along the path. The centripetal part \(v^2/r\) is still there. The total acceleration has size \(\sqrt{a_t^2 + a_c^2}\) and points ahead of the center when speeding up, behind it when slowing down.

Key idea

Centripetal acceleration (m/s², toward the center) changes the direction of the velocity. Angular acceleration \(\alpha\) (rad/s², along the axis) changes the rate of turning, and shows up for a point as the tangential acceleration \(r\alpha\).
